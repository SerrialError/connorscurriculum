Dimensional analysis carries the **units** through every step of a calculation. If the units work out
to what the question asks for, the setup is almost always right. Chapter 3's mole calculations, and most
calculations after that, use the same method.

## 1. Conversion factors (1.7)

A **conversion factor** is a fraction whose top and bottom are equal amounts in different units, so it
equals 1. From $1\ \text{in} = 2.54\ \text{cm}$ you get two factors:

$$
\frac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{in}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{1\ \text{in}}{2.54\ \text{cm}}
$$

Multiplying by either one doesn't change the quantity, only its units. Pick the one that puts the unit
you want to **cancel** on the bottom:

$$
\text{given units} \times \frac{\text{desired units}}{\text{given units}} = \text{desired units}
$$

<Reveal label="Worked example: one factor">

Convert 8.50 in to centimeters.

$$
8.50\ \cancel{\text{in}} \times \frac{2.54\ \text{cm}}{1\ \cancel{\text{in}}} = 21.6\ \text{cm}
$$

2.54 cm and 1 in are exact (a definition), so the answer keeps the 3 significant figures of 8.50.

</Reveal>

## 2. Chaining factors (1.7)

When there's no single factor from the starting unit to the final one, chain several, canceling one unit
at a time.

<Reveal label="Worked example: speed in different units">

A car travels at 65.0 mi/h. What is its speed in meters per second? (1 mi = 1.609 km)

Convert miles to meters on top, and hours to seconds on the bottom:

$$
\frac{65.0\ \cancel{\text{mi}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{h}}} \times \frac{1.609\ \cancel{\text{km}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{mi}}} \times \frac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \cancel{\text{km}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{h}}}{60\ \cancel{\text{min}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{min}}}{60\ \text{s}} = 29.1\ \text{m/s}
$$

</Reveal>

After calculating, check that the units are the ones asked for and that the size makes sense: 1 mi/h
is about 0.45 m/s, so 65 mi/h should be roughly 29 m/s.

## 3. Squared and cubed units (1.7)

When a unit is squared or cubed, the **conversion factor is squared or cubed too**, numbers and
units together:

$$
1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 1\ \text{m}^3 = (100\ \text{cm})^3 = 10^6\ \text{cm}^3
$$

<Callout type="warning">
A common mistake is to convert $\text{m}^3$ to $\text{cm}^3$ by multiplying by 100. A cubic meter is
a million cubic centimeters, not a hundred. Cube the whole factor:
$\left(\dfrac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^3$.
</Callout>

<Reveal label="Worked example: a cubed conversion">

A room has a volume of 52.0 m³. What is its volume in liters?

$$
52.0\ \cancel{\text{m}^3} \times \left(\frac{100\ \cancel{\text{cm}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{m}}}\right)^3 \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{mL}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{cm}^3}} \times \frac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} = 5.20 \times 10^4\ \text{L}
$$

A shortcut is $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000\ \text{L}$, since $1\ \text{L} = 1\ \text{dm}^3$ and a meter is 10 dm.

</Reveal>

## 4. Density as a conversion factor

Any quantity that relates two units can be a conversion factor. Density relates mass and volume, so it
can be used either way up:

$$
\frac{0.789\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{mL}} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \frac{1\ \text{mL}}{0.789\ \text{g}}
$$

<Reveal label="Worked example: volume from mass">

Ethanol has a density of 0.789 g/mL. What volume of ethanol, in liters, has a mass of 1.50 kg?

$$
1.50\ \cancel{\text{kg}} \times \frac{1000\ \cancel{\text{g}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{kg}}} \times \frac{1\ \cancel{\text{mL}}}{0.789\ \cancel{\text{g}}} \times \frac{1\ \text{L}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{mL}}} = 1.90\ \text{L}
$$

Ethanol is less dense than water, so 1.50 kg should take up more than 1.50 L, as it does.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: density in different units">

Mercury has a density of 13.6 g/cm³. Express it in kg/m³.

$$
\frac{13.6\ \cancel{\text{g}}}{1\ \cancel{\text{cm}^3}} \times \frac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \cancel{\text{g}}} \times \left(\frac{100\ \cancel{\text{cm}}}{1\ \text{m}}\right)^3 = 1.36 \times 10^4\ \text{kg/m}^3
$$

</Reveal>

## 5. A problem-solving routine

1. Write down what's given (with units) and what's asked for.
2. Plan the path of units from one to the other.
3. Set up every factor so the unwanted unit cancels.
4. Calculate, then round to the right number of significant figures. Exact factors (definitions like
   1000 g = 1 kg) don't limit the significant figures.
5. Check the units and whether the size of the answer is sensible.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
The AP exam never asks for unit conversion on its own, but almost every calculation uses it: grams to
moles, mL to L for molarity, °C to K for gases. Writing units on every number is the easiest way to
catch a setup mistake on a free-response question.
</Callout>
