## 1. The periodic table (2.5)

The periodic table lists the elements in order of **atomic number**, in rows arranged so that elements
with similar properties line up in columns.

- A row is a **period** (7 in all).
- A column is a **group** (or family), numbered 1–18. Elements in a group have similar chemistry.
  Groups 1, 2, and 13–18 are the **main-group** elements; groups 3–12 are the **transition metals**.

![The periodic table with metals shaded blue, nonmetals green, and metalloids (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, At) red along a staircase line from boron down to astatine. Groups 1, 2, 17, and 18 are labeled alkali metals, alkaline earth metals, halogens, and noble gases. H, N, O, F, Cl, Br, and I are outlined in bold as the diatomic elements.](./periodic-table-regions.svg)

| Group | Name | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **alkali metals** | Li, Na, K |
| 2 | **alkaline earth metals** | Mg, Ca, Ba |
| 16 | **chalcogens** | O, S, Se |
| 17 | **halogens** | F, Cl, Br, I |
| 18 | **noble gases** | He, Ne, Ar |

Hydrogen sits in group 1 but is a **nonmetal**.

### Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids

A **staircase** line running from boron down to astatine divides the table:

- **Metals** (left of the line, most elements): shiny, conduct heat and electricity, malleable. All are
  solids at room temperature except mercury.
- **Nonmetals** (upper right): dull, poor conductors. Several are gases; bromine is a liquid.
- **Metalloids** (along the line): in-between properties. Silicon conducts electricity, but much less
  than a metal, which is why it's used in semiconductors.

## 2. Molecules and chemical formulas (2.6)

A **molecule** is two or more atoms bonded tightly together. A **chemical formula** gives the atoms in it,
with subscripts for how many of each.

Seven elements exist as **diatomic molecules** in their usual form: $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{O2}$,
$\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$, and $\ce{I2}$. On the table they make a "7" starting at nitrogen,
plus hydrogen. Oxygen also forms **ozone**, $\ce{O3}$, and sulfur and phosphorus form $\ce{S8}$ and
$\ce{P4}$ molecules.

Compounds made of molecules are **molecular compounds**. They usually contain only **nonmetals**:
$\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{CH4}$.

### Molecular and empirical formulas

- A **molecular formula** gives the actual number of each atom in one molecule.
- An **empirical formula** gives only the **smallest whole-number ratio** of the atoms.

| Compound | Molecular formula | Empirical formula |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogen peroxide | $\ce{H2O2}$ | $\ce{HO}$ |
| glucose | $\ce{C6H12O6}$ | $\ce{CH2O}$ |
| ethylene | $\ce{C2H4}$ | $\ce{CH2}$ |
| water | $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{H2O}$ (already lowest terms) |

Different compounds can share an empirical formula: ethylene ($\ce{C2H4}$) and cyclohexane ($\ce{C6H12}$)
are both $\ce{CH2}$. Mass data (Chapter 3) give the empirical formula; the molar mass is needed to get
the molecular formula.

### Picturing molecules

A **structural formula** shows which atoms are bonded to which: H–O–O–H for hydrogen peroxide. It
usually doesn't show the 3D shape. **Ball-and-stick** and **space-filling** models do; Chapter 9
explains the shapes.

## 3. Ions (2.7)

An **ion** is an atom or group of atoms with a net charge, because the number of electrons doesn't
equal the number of protons.

- A **cation** is positive: it has **lost** electrons. Metals form cations.
- An **anion** is negative: it has **gained** electrons. Nonmetals form anions.

### Predicting charges from the table

Main-group elements tend to gain or lose electrons until they have the **same number of electrons as the
nearest noble gas**:

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion charge | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ (Al) | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Examples | $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$ | $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | $\ce{Al^3+}$ | $\ce{N^3-}$, $\ce{P^3-}$ | $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{S^2-}$ | $\ce{F-}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ |

$\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{N^3-}$, $\ce{O^2-}$, and $\ce{F-}$ all have 10
electrons, the same as neon. Chapters 6–8 explain why with electron configurations and energy.

Transition metals often form **more than one** cation ($\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, $\ce{Cu+}$ and
$\ce{Cu^2+}$), so their charges can't be read off the group number. A few are worth knowing:
$\ce{Zn^2+}$ and $\ce{Ag+}$ form only one ion.

### Polyatomic ions

A **polyatomic ion** is a group of bonded atoms with an overall charge. The group stays together through
reactions. The common ones:

| Ion | Name | Ion | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NH4+}$ | ammonium | $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | carbonate |
| $\ce{OH-}$ | hydroxide | $\ce{HCO3-}$ | hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate) |
| $\ce{NO3-}$ | nitrate | $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | sulfate |
| $\ce{NO2-}$ | nitrite | $\ce{SO3^2-}$ | sulfite |
| $\ce{CN-}$ | cyanide | $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | phosphate |
| $\ce{C2H3O2-}$ | acetate | $\ce{MnO4-}$ | permanganate |
| $\ce{ClO4-}$ | perchlorate | $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ | dichromate |
| $\ce{ClO3-}$ | chlorate | $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ | chromate |

The next lesson has the naming patterns that let you work out most of these from a few.

## 4. Ionic compounds (2.7)

An **ionic compound** is made of cations and anions held together by their attraction. It usually
forms between a **metal and a nonmetal** (or contains a polyatomic ion). It isn't made of molecules: a
crystal of NaCl is a huge array of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions, and the formula only gives their
ratio. The smallest unit with that ratio is a **formula unit**.

Ionic formulas are always **empirical formulas**. Write them so the **total charge is zero**:

1. Write the cation, then the anion, with their charges.
2. Find the smallest whole-number ratio that makes the charges cancel. A quick way: each ion's charge
   number becomes the other ion's subscript, then reduce.
3. Put parentheses around a polyatomic ion that needs a subscript.

| Ions | Formula | Charge check |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ | $\ce{MgCl2}$ | $+2 + 2(-1) = 0$ |
| $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{O^2-}$ | $\ce{Al2O3}$ | $2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0$ |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{O^2-}$ | $\ce{CaO}$ (not $\ce{Ca2O2}$) | $+2 - 2 = 0$ |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{PO4^3-}$ | $\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}$ | $3(+2) + 2(-3) = 0$ |
| $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$ | $2(+1) - 2 = 0$ |

<Callout type="warning">
Subscripts inside a polyatomic ion can't change. Calcium hydroxide is $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, two hydroxide
ions. Writing $\ce{CaOH2}$ would mean one O and two H.
</Callout>

<Reveal label="Worked example: counting atoms in a formula">

How many atoms of each element are in one formula unit of $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$, and how many ions?

The subscript 3 multiplies everything inside the parentheses:

- Al: 2
- S: $1 \times 3 = 3$
- O: $4 \times 3 = 12$

That's 17 atoms in 5 ions: 2 $\ce{Al^3+}$ and 3 $\ce{SO4^2-}$. Charges: $2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0$.

</Reveal>

## 5. Ionic or molecular?

| | Ionic | Molecular |
|---|---|---|
| Usually made of | metal + nonmetal, or a polyatomic ion | only nonmetals |
| Particles | ions in a lattice | separate molecules |
| Formula | empirical (lowest ratio) | molecular (actual count) |
| Example | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{K2CO3}$, $\ce{NH4Cl}$ | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{N2O4}$, $\ce{C6H12O6}$ |

$\ce{NH4Cl}$ is ionic even though it has only nonmetals, because it's made of $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions.

electrons, which Chapter 6 explains.
</Callout>
