This page is the short version of the Chapter 1–3 lessons.

## What the teacher said about the test

- **Format:** 45 multiple-choice questions.
- **About 20 are math.** Some are very easy (a unit conversion, grams to moles), and some take
  several steps, like **empirical formulas** and **limiting and excess reactants**.
- The rest test the ideas: classifying matter, physical and chemical changes, atomic structure,
  isotopes, ions, and names and formulas.

To practice in the same format, take a [practice exam](../../practice/?test=ch1-3-test&exam=1&start=1):
45 multiple-choice questions, about 20 of them calculations, drawn fresh each time. It always
includes empirical-formula and limiting-reactant problems.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On the AP exam these chapters are mostly Unit 1 (moles, mass spectra, empirical formulas, mixtures) and
the stoichiometry in Unit 4. Significant figures, unit conversions, and naming aren't tested on their
own, but every AP calculation and question uses them.
</Callout>

## What to memorize

The equation sheet doesn't give you these:

| Know | Formula or rule |
|---|---|
| Significant figures | ×, ÷: fewest sig figs. +, −: fewest decimal places. |
| Percent composition | $\dfrac{(\text{atoms of element})(\text{atomic weight})}{\text{formula weight}} \times 100\%$ |
| Average atomic mass | $\sum (\text{isotope mass})(\text{fractional abundance})$ |
| Percent yield | $\dfrac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100\%$ |
| Ion charges | group 1: 1+, group 2: 2+, Al: 3+, group 15: 3−, group 16: 2−, group 17: 1− |
| Polyatomic ions | name, formula, and charge of the 16 in [The Periodic Table, Molecules, and Ions](../02-periodic-table-molecules-and-ions/#polyatomic-ions) |

The textbook also covers Fahrenheit, $R_f$ values, calories, mass spectra of molecules, and naming
organic compounds (Section 2.9). None of them are on this test or the AP exam, so the lessons leave
them out.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
The AP course description has no official list of polyatomic ions, but exam questions often name a
compound (silver nitrate, sodium bicarbonate, ammonium chloride) and expect you to write its formula.
Know the 16 in the table by heart, plus hydronium, $\ce{H3O+}$, which comes back in the acid–base
chapters. The rest you can work out from the [naming patterns](../02-naming-compounds/): chlorite
and hypochlorite from chlorate, and hydrogen sulfate or dihydrogen phosphate from sulfate and phosphate.
</Callout>

## Calculations to be able to do

Each links to the lesson with worked examples.

**Chapter 1**

- Convert units with conversion factors, including squared and cubed units (cm³ to m³ is $10^6$, not 100).
  [Dimensional Analysis](../01-dimensional-analysis/)
- Density, and using it to go between mass and volume. °C to K.
  [Units, Measurement, and Significant Figures](../01-measurement-and-significant-figures/)
- Count significant figures and round answers correctly.

**Chapter 2**

- Count protons, neutrons, and electrons in an atom or ion from its symbol.
  [Atomic Theory and the Structure of the Atom](../02-atomic-structure/)
- Average atomic mass from isotope masses and abundances, and the reverse (two isotopes, solve for $x$).
  [Atomic Weights and Mass Spectra](../02-atomic-weights-and-mass-spectra/)

**Chapter 3**

- Formula weight, percent composition, and grams ↔ moles ↔ particles.
  [Formula Weights, the Mole, and Molar Mass](../03-the-mole-and-molar-mass/)
- Empirical formula from percents or masses, molecular formula from molar mass, combustion
  analysis, and hydrates. [Empirical Formulas](../03-empirical-formulas/)
- Grams of one substance from grams of another, the limiting reactant, how much excess is left, and
  percent yield. [Stoichiometry and Limiting Reactants](../03-stoichiometry-and-limiting-reactants/)

### The two long problems, step by step

<Reveal label="Empirical formula">

1. Percent → grams (assume 100 g).
2. Grams → moles (÷ atomic weight).
3. Divide every mole value by the smallest.
4. If a ratio ends in about .5, .33, or .25, multiply all of them by 2, 3, or 4.
5. For a molecular formula: molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass = the multiplier.

For combustion analysis, mol C = mol $\ce{CO2}$, mol H = **2 ×** mol $\ce{H2O}$, and oxygen comes
from subtraction.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Limiting and excess reactant">

1. Convert each reactant's grams to moles.
2. From each one, calculate how much product it could make (use the mole ratio).
3. The reactant that makes **less** is limiting, and that amount is the theoretical yield.
4. Excess left over: moles of excess used = moles of limiting × mole ratio; subtract from what you
   started with, then convert to grams.
5. Check: total mass before = mass of product + excess left (for a reaction with one product).

</Reveal>

## Theory checklist

Be able to explain each in a sentence or two:

- How to tell an element, a compound, and a mixture apart, including from a particle diagram. A
  molecule of an element ($\ce{O2}$) is still an element.
- Physical vs. chemical properties and changes, and intensive vs. extensive properties.
- At the particle level: a chemical change breaks and forms bonds; melting or boiling only changes
  the attractions between molecules.
- How filtration, distillation, and chromatography each separate a mixture, and why filtration can't
  separate a solution.
- Kinetic vs. potential energy, and why opposite charges have lower energy when closer together.
- Precision vs. accuracy, and exact vs. measured numbers.
- Dalton's atomic theory, which part turned out to be wrong, and the laws of conservation of mass,
  constant composition, and multiple proportions.
- What the cathode-ray, oil-drop, and gold-foil experiments each showed.
- What isotopes are, and what a mass spectrum's peaks, positions, and heights mean.
- Groups, periods, metals, nonmetals, metalloids, and the seven diatomic elements.
- Molecular vs. empirical formulas, and ionic vs. molecular compounds.
- Naming ionic compounds (Roman numerals for transition metals), oxyanions (per-, -ate, -ite,
  hypo-), acids (hydro-ic, -ic, -ous), and molecular compounds (Greek prefixes).
- Balancing equations, and combination, decomposition, and combustion reactions.
- The mole, Avogadro's number, and why molar mass in g/mol equals formula weight in amu.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Instead |
|---|---|
| Converting m³ to cm³ by multiplying by 100 | Cube the factor: $(100)^3 = 10^6$ |
| Counting leading zeros (0.0045 has 4 sig figs) | Leading zeros never count: 2 sig figs |
| Using sig figs, not decimal places, when adding | Addition keeps the fewest **decimal places** |
| Plain average of isotope masses | Weight each by its abundance |
| Roman numeral = number of metal atoms | It's the charge on **one** metal ion: $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is iron(III) |
| Prefixes on ionic names ("magnesium dichloride") | Prefixes only for two nonmetals: magnesium chloride |
| Changing subscripts to balance | Change coefficients only |
| Comparing grams across an equation | Convert to moles first; coefficients are mole ratios |
| Forgetting to double mol $\ce{H2O}$ for mol H | Each $\ce{H2O}$ has 2 H |
| Rounding 1.33 or 2.5 to a whole number | Multiply by 3 or 2 to get whole numbers |
| Picking the limiting reactant by grams | Compare the product each reactant can make |
