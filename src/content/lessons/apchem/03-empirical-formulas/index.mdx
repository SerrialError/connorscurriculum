The last lesson went from a formula to percent composition. This one goes the other way: from
measured masses to a formula. It works because of the law of constant composition: a pure compound
always has the same ratio of elements by mass.

## 1. Empirical formula from mass percents (3.5)

The subscripts in a formula are **mole ratios** of atoms, so convert masses to moles and compare:

1. **Percent to mass.** Assume a 100 g sample, so each percent becomes grams.
2. **Mass to moles.** Divide each mass by the element's atomic weight.
3. **Divide by the smallest** number of moles.
4. **Make whole numbers.** If a ratio is close to a fraction, multiply all of them by the same small
   integer.

| Ratio ends in about | Multiply by |
|---|---|
| .5 | 2 |
| .33 or .67 | 3 |
| .25 or .75 | 4 |

"Percent to mass, mass to mole, divide by small, multiply till whole."

<Reveal label="Worked example: an oxide of phosphorus">

A compound is 43.64% P and 56.36% O by mass. Find its empirical formula.

| | Mass in 100 g | Moles | ÷ smallest |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 43.64 g | $43.64 / 30.97 = 1.409$ | 1.000 |
| O | 56.36 g | $56.36 / 16.00 = 3.523$ | 2.500 |

The ratio 1 : 2.5 isn't whole, so double it: 2 : 5. The empirical formula is $\ce{P2O5}$.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: a ratio of 1.33">

An iron oxide is 72.36% Fe. Find its empirical formula.

The rest is oxygen: 27.64%.

- Fe: $72.36 / 55.85 = 1.296$ mol
- O: $27.64 / 16.00 = 1.728$ mol

Divide by 1.296: Fe 1.000, O 1.333. Multiply by 3: Fe 3, O 4. The formula is $\ce{Fe3O4}$.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
Don't round 1.33 to 1 or 2.5 to 3. A ratio within about 0.1 of a whole number can be rounded; anything
else means you need to multiply. Rounding too soon in the moles step can also hide the right ratio, so
keep 3–4 significant figures until the end.
</Callout>

## 2. Molecular formula from the empirical formula (3.5)

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula. If you know the molar mass:

$$
n = \frac{\text{molar mass of the compound}}{\text{mass of one empirical formula unit}} \qquad \text{molecular formula} = (\text{empirical formula})_n
$$

<Reveal label="Worked example: from P₂O₅ to the molecule">

The phosphorus oxide above has a molar mass of about 284 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

Empirical formula mass of $\ce{P2O5}$ $= 2(30.97) + 5(16.00) = 141.94$ g/mol.

$n = 284 / 141.94 = 2.00$, so the molecular formula is $\ce{P4O10}$.

</Reveal>

## 3. Empirical formula from masses

When a problem gives masses instead of percents, skip step 1 and convert the grams directly.

<Reveal label="Worked example: a hydrate">

A 2.50 g sample of a copper(II) sulfate hydrate, $\ce{CuSO4.xH2O}$, is heated until all the water is
gone. The anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ left behind has a mass of 1.60 g. Find $x$.

Water lost $= 2.50 - 1.60 = 0.90$ g.

- $\ce{CuSO4}$: $1.60 / 159.61 = 0.01002$ mol
- $\ce{H2O}$: $0.90 / 18.02 = 0.050$ mol

Ratio $= 0.050 / 0.01002 = 5.0$. The hydrate is $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$, copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate.

If the sample isn't heated long enough, some water stays behind. The measured water mass is then too
low, and so is $x$.

</Reveal>

## 4. Combustion analysis (3.5)

For compounds of carbon and hydrogen (and often oxygen), the sample is burned in excess oxygen.
**All the carbon ends up in $\ce{CO2}$ and all the hydrogen ends up in $\ce{H2O}$**, which are trapped
and weighed.

![Combustion analysis apparatus. O₂ flows into a furnace where the sample burns. The gases pass through an H₂O absorber, then a CO₂ absorber. The gain in mass of each absorber gives the grams of H₂O and CO₂ formed. Oxygen in the sample is found by subtraction.](./combustion-analysis.svg)

1. Moles of C = moles of $\ce{CO2}$ (one C per $\ce{CO2}$).
2. Moles of H = **2 ×** moles of $\ce{H2O}$ (two H per $\ce{H2O}$).
3. If the compound contains O, find its mass **by subtraction**: sample mass − mass of C − mass of H.
   (The O in the products came partly from the added $\ce{O2}$, so it can't be used directly.)
4. Find the mole ratio as before.

<Reveal label="Worked example: a compound of C, H, and O">

Burning 0.2550 g of a compound containing C, H, and O gives 0.5602 g of $\ce{CO2}$ and 0.3058 g of
$\ce{H2O}$. Find the empirical formula.

**Carbon:** $0.5602 / 44.01 = 0.01273$ mol $\ce{CO2}$, so 0.01273 mol C, which is
$0.01273 \times 12.01 = 0.1529$ g C.

**Hydrogen:** $0.3058 / 18.02 = 0.01697$ mol $\ce{H2O}$, so $2 \times 0.01697 = 0.03394$ mol H, which is
$0.03394 \times 1.008 = 0.03421$ g H.

**Oxygen:** $0.2550 - 0.1529 - 0.0342 = 0.0679$ g O, which is $0.0679 / 16.00 = 0.00424$ mol O.

**Ratio** (÷ 0.00424): C 3.00, H 8.00, O 1.00. The empirical formula is $\ce{C3H8O}$.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
Two mistakes are common here: forgetting to **double** the moles of $\ce{H2O}$ to get moles of H, and
taking the oxygen from the $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ instead of by subtraction.
</Callout>

## 5. Mixtures versus pure substances

A pure compound has one fixed composition. A **mixture**'s composition depends on how much of each
component is in it. The **mass percent** of a component in a mixture is

$$
\text{mass percent} = \frac{\text{mass of component}}{\text{total mass of mixture}} \times 100\%
$$

A 1.25 g tablet that contains 0.500 g of vitamin C is 40.0% vitamin C by mass. Another tablet from a
different brand might be 60%; both are still mixtures.

## 6. Using composition to check purity

**Elemental analysis** measures the mass percent of an element in a sample. Compare it with the
percent expected for the pure compound:

- If it matches, the sample is likely pure.
- If it doesn't, something else is mixed in. Whether the measured percent is **higher or lower**
  tells you about the impurity.

The rule: an impurity pulls the measured percent toward **its own** percent of that element. An
impurity with **less** of the element (or none) lowers the measured value; one with **more** raises it.

<Reveal label="Worked example: higher or lower?">

A sample of NaCl is contaminated with a small amount of NaI. Is its measured mass percent of sodium
higher or lower than in pure NaCl?

- Pure NaCl: $22.99 / 58.44 = 39.34\%$ Na
- NaI: $22.99 / 149.89 = 15.34\%$ Na

NaI has a much smaller percent of Na (iodine is heavy), so the mixture's percent of Na is **lower**
than 39.34%.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: how much of each?">

A white powder is a mixture of NaCl and KCl. Analysis shows it's 55.0% chlorine by mass. What percent of
the mixture is NaCl?

Pure NaCl is $35.45 / 58.44 = 60.66\%$ Cl. Pure KCl is $35.45 / 74.55 = 47.55\%$ Cl.

Let $x$ be the mass fraction of NaCl:

$$
60.66x + 47.55(1 - x) = 55.0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 13.11x = 7.45 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 0.568
$$

The mixture is about **56.8% NaCl** and 43.2% KCl. As a check, 55.0% is between 47.55% and 60.66%,
a bit closer to NaCl's value.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="ap-tip">
AP topic 1.4 usually asks this qualitatively: given a measured mass percent, decide which impurity
could explain it, or whether a result was too high or too low. Compare each possible impurity's
percent of the element with the pure compound's.
</Callout>
