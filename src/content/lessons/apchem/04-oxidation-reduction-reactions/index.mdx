## 1. Oxidation and reduction (4.4)

In an **oxidation–reduction (redox)** reaction, electrons move from one substance to another.

- **Oxidation** is the **loss** of electrons.
- **Reduction** is the **gain** of electrons.

A memory aid: **OIL RIG**, "oxidation is loss, reduction is gain." The two always happen together:
electrons lost by one substance are gained by another.

Drop a strip of zinc into blue copper(II) sulfate solution, and reddish copper metal coats the zinc while
the blue color fades:

![A zinc strip in a beaker of Cu²⁺ solution, with copper metal forming on the strip and Zn²⁺ ions in solution. Beside it: oxidation, Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻, oxidation number 0 to +2; reduction, Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, oxidation number +2 to 0. Added together the electrons cancel: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).](./zn-cu-transfer.svg)

Zinc atoms lose electrons (oxidized) and copper(II) ions gain them (reduced). The sulfate ions are
spectators.

## 2. Oxidation numbers (4.4)

For ionic compounds, the charges show where the electrons went. For molecules and polyatomic ions,
chemists use **oxidation numbers** (also called oxidation states): the charge an atom would have if all
its bonds were ionic. Assign them in this order:

1. An atom in an **element** is 0: Na, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{P4}$.
2. A **monatomic ion** equals its charge: $\ce{Na+}$ is +1, $\ce{S^2-}$ is −2.
3. **Fluorine** is always −1 in a compound.
4. **Oxygen** is usually −2. (In peroxides such as $\ce{H2O2}$ it's −1.)
5. **Hydrogen** is +1 with nonmetals and −1 with metals (NaH).
6. The other halogens are −1, except when bonded to O or a halogen above them (in $\ce{ClO3-}$, Cl is positive).
7. The oxidation numbers add up to the **overall charge**: 0 for a neutral compound, or the ion's charge.

<Reveal label="Worked example: oxidation numbers in compounds and ions">

**S in $\ce{SO4^2-}$:** $x + 4(-2) = -2$, so $x = +6$.

**Cr in $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$:** $2x + 7(-2) = -2$, so $2x = 12$ and $x = +6$.

**N in $\ce{NH4+}$:** $x + 4(+1) = +1$, so $x = -3$.

**C in $\ce{CH4}$ and in $\ce{CO2}$:** $x + 4(+1) = 0$ gives −4; $x + 2(-2) = 0$ gives +4.

**Mn in $\ce{KMnO4}$:** K is +1 (group 1), so $+1 + x + 4(-2) = 0$ and $x = +7$.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="note">
An oxidation number is a bookkeeping number, not a real charge. Carbon in $\ce{CO2}$ is "+4," but the
molecule has no $\ce{C^4+}$ ion in it. Write oxidation numbers with the sign first (+4), and ion charges
with the sign after (4+).
</Callout>

## 3. Finding what's oxidized and reduced (4.4)

Assign oxidation numbers on both sides and look for changes:

- the oxidation number **goes up** → that element is **oxidized** (it lost electrons),
- the oxidation number **goes down** → that element is **reduced** (it gained electrons).

<Reveal label="Worked example: methane burning">

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}
$$

- C: −4 in $\ce{CH4}$ → +4 in $\ce{CO2}$. Up 8: **carbon is oxidized**.
- O: 0 in $\ce{O2}$ → −2 in $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. Down: **oxygen is reduced**.
- H: +1 → +1. No change.

Every combustion reaction is a redox reaction for the same reason: $\ce{O2}$ (oxidation number 0)
ends up as oxygen at −2.

</Reveal>

If no oxidation number changes, it isn't a redox reaction. In
$\ce{AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3}$, Ag stays +1, Cl −1, Na +1, N +5, and O −2: it's precipitation.
In $\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}$, nothing changes either: it's acid–base.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
A quick screen for redox: is there an **element by itself** (like Zn, $\ce{O2}$, or $\ce{H2}$) on either
side? An uncombined element has oxidation number 0, and it almost always ends up in a compound at a
different number, so the reaction is redox. Confirm with the numbers.
</Callout>

## 4. Metals with acids and salts (4.4)

Many metals are oxidized by acids, which releases hydrogen gas:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Mg^2+(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

Mg goes from 0 to +2 (oxidized), and H from +1 to 0 (reduced). $\ce{Cl-}$ is a spectator.

A metal can also be oxidized by the ions of another metal in a salt solution, as zinc was by
$\ce{Cu^2+}$ above. Both are **displacement** reactions: the metal pushes another element (H or a metal)
out of its compound.

## 5. The activity series (4.4)

Whether a displacement reaction happens depends on which metal gives up electrons more easily. The
**activity series** lists metals from most easily oxidized to least:

![A row of metals from most to least active: Li, K, Ba, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Mn, Zn, Cr, Fe, Co, Ni, Sn, Pb, then H₂, then Cu, Ag, Hg, Pt, Au. Metals before H₂ react with acids such as HCl to give H₂ gas; metals after it don't. A metal is oxidized by the ions of any metal after it, so Fe(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Fe²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) happens, and Cu(s) + Fe²⁺(aq) gives no reaction.](./activity-series.svg)

- A metal reacts with the ions of **any metal below it** (to its right in the figure).
- Metals **above hydrogen** react with acids like HCl to make $\ce{H2}$. Copper, silver, and gold don't.

<Reveal label="Worked example: will it react?">

Use the activity series to predict whether each reaction happens. Write the net ionic equation if it does.

**(a) Nickel metal in silver nitrate solution.** Ni is above Ag, so Ni is oxidized:

$$
\ce{Ni(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Ni^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}
$$

The 2 in front of $\ce{Ag+}$ balances the charge: $+2$ on each side.

**(b) Silver metal in nickel(II) nitrate solution.** Ag is below Ni: **no reaction**.

**(c) Zinc in hydrochloric acid.** Zn is above $\ce{H2}$:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

</Reveal>

<Callout type="note">
The AP exam doesn't ask you to recall the activity series. A question gives you the order or the
information it needs. What it does expect is using an order you're given to predict a reaction.
</Callout>

## 6. Half-reactions (AP topic 4.9)

A redox reaction can be split into two **half-reactions**, one for the oxidation and one for the
reduction, each showing the electrons:

$$
\text{oxidation: } \ce{Al(s) -> Al^3+(aq) + 3e-}
$$

$$
\text{reduction: } \ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}
$$

To combine them, the electrons lost must equal the electrons gained. Multiply each half-reaction so the
electrons match, then add and cancel:

$$
2 \times (\ce{Al -> Al^3+ + 3e-}) \quad\text{and}\quad 3 \times (\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu})
$$

$$
\ce{2Al(s) + 3Cu^2+(aq) -> 2Al^3+(aq) + 3Cu(s)}
$$

Six electrons are lost and six gained. Check the charge: $3(+2) = +6$ on the left and $2(+3) = +6$ on the
right.

<Callout type="warning">
A redox equation can have equal atoms on both sides and still be wrong. $\ce{Al + Cu^2+ -> Al^3+ + Cu}$
has one Al and one Cu on each side, but the charge is +2 on the left and +3 on the right. Always check
the charge in a redox equation.
</Callout>

Chapter 20 extends half-reactions to reactions in acidic solution, where $\ce{H2O}$ and $\ce{H+}$ have to
be added to balance oxygen and hydrogen, and to batteries.
