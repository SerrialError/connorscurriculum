## 1. Stoichiometry with solutions (4.6)

Chapter 3 converted grams to moles with molar mass. For a solution, **molarity × volume** gives moles.
After that, the problem is the same: the mole ratio from the balanced equation connects the two
substances.

![A map. M × L of a solution of A, or grams of solid A divided by its molar mass, gives moles of A. Multiplying by the mole ratio B/A gives moles of B. From moles of B, divide by M to get liters of a solution of B, or multiply by molar mass to get grams of solid B.](./solution-stoichiometry-map.svg)

<Reveal label="Worked example: volume needed to neutralize an acid">

What volume of 0.150 M NaOH neutralizes 25.0 mL of 0.200 M $\ce{H2SO4}$?

$$
\ce{H2SO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)}
$$

Moles of $\ce{H2SO4}$: $0.0250\ \text{L} \times 0.200\ M = 0.00500$ mol

Moles of NaOH: $0.00500 \times \tfrac{2}{1} = 0.0100$ mol

$$
V = \frac{0.0100\ \text{mol}}{0.150\ \text{mol/L}} = 0.0667\ \text{L} = 66.7\ \text{mL}
$$

Using $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ here gives 33.3 mL, which is wrong. That shortcut is for dilution. It ignores
the 1 : 2 ratio in the equation.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: mass of a precipitate">

Excess $\ce{Na2SO4}$ solution is added to 40.0 mL of 0.100 M $\ce{BaCl2}$. What mass of $\ce{BaSO4}$
precipitates?

$$
\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}
$$

Moles of $\ce{Ba^2+}$: $0.0400\ \text{L} \times 0.100\ M = 0.00400$ mol, giving 0.00400 mol $\ce{BaSO4}$.

$\mathcal{M}(\ce{BaSO4}) = 137.33 + 32.06 + 4(16.00) = 233.39$ g/mol

$$
0.00400\ \text{mol} \times 233.39\ \text{g/mol} = 0.934\ \text{g}
$$

Measuring an unknown amount by weighing a precipitate like this is called **gravimetric analysis**.

</Reveal>

## 2. Limiting reactants in solution (4.6)

When neither solution is in excess, find the limiting reactant from moles, as in Chapter 3. Then the ions
left over, and the spectators, are spread through the **combined** volume.

<Reveal label="Worked example: silver chloride (hard)">

50.0 mL of 0.100 M $\ce{AgNO3}$ is mixed with 30.0 mL of 0.200 M NaCl.

**(a)** What mass of AgCl forms? **(b)** What are the concentrations of the ions left in solution?

**Moles:** $\ce{Ag+}$: $0.0500 \times 0.100 = 0.00500$ mol. $\ce{Cl-}$: $0.0300 \times 0.200 = 0.00600$ mol.

The ratio is 1 : 1, so **$\ce{Ag+}$ is limiting**, and 0.00500 mol of AgCl forms.

**(a)** $0.00500 \times 143.32 = 0.717$ g AgCl.

**(b)** Total volume $= 50.0 + 30.0 = 80.0$ mL $= 0.0800$ L.

- $\ce{Cl-}$ left: $0.00600 - 0.00500 = 0.00100$ mol, so $[\ce{Cl-}] = 0.00100 / 0.0800 = 0.0125\ M$
- $\ce{Na+}$ (spectator): $0.00600 / 0.0800 = 0.0750\ M$
- $\ce{NO3-}$ (spectator): $0.00500 / 0.0800 = 0.0625\ M$
- $[\ce{Ag+}] \approx 0$: it was all used up.

Check the charge balance: $0.0750 = 0.0125 + 0.0625$. Positive and negative charge match.

</Reveal>

## 3. Titration (4.6)

A **titration** finds the amount of a substance (the **analyte**) by reacting it with a solution of
known concentration (the **titrant**), added slowly from a buret until the reaction is just complete.

![A buret clamped above an Erlenmeyer flask. The buret holds the titrant, of known concentration such as 0.100 M NaOH; read its volume before and after. The flask holds a measured volume of analyte, of unknown concentration, with a few drops of indicator. Equivalence point: the moles of titrant added are exactly what the balanced equation needs for the analyte. End point: the indicator changes color, pink for phenolphthalein in base.](./titration-setup.svg)

- The **equivalence point** is where the titrant added exactly uses up the analyte, in the ratio of the
  balanced equation.
- The **end point** is where you *see* the change, usually an **indicator** changing color. A good
  indicator makes the end point fall very close to the equivalence point.
- A solution whose concentration is known precisely is a **standard solution**.

Phenolphthalein is colorless in acid and pink in base. In an acid titrated with NaOH, the first drop of
extra base turns the whole flask faint pink.

<Reveal label="Worked example: concentration of an acid">

25.00 mL of HCl solution needs 31.20 mL of 0.1050 M NaOH to reach the end point. What is the
concentration of the HCl?

$$
\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

Moles of NaOH: $0.03120\ \text{L} \times 0.1050\ M = 0.003276$ mol

1 : 1 ratio, so 0.003276 mol HCl in 25.00 mL:

$$
[\ce{HCl}] = \frac{0.003276\ \text{mol}}{0.02500\ \text{L}} = 0.1310\ M
$$

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: molar mass of an unknown acid">

0.2040 g of a solid monoprotic acid is dissolved in water and titrated with 0.0750 M NaOH. The end
point is reached after 18.30 mL. What is the acid's molar mass?

Moles of NaOH $= 0.01830 \times 0.0750 = 0.001373$ mol. A monoprotic acid reacts 1 : 1, so the sample
contained 0.001373 mol of acid.

$$
\mathcal{M} = \frac{0.2040\ \text{g}}{0.001373\ \text{mol}} = 149\ \text{g/mol}
$$

The answer has three significant figures because 0.0750 M has three.

</Reveal>

Titrations aren't only for acids and bases. A redox titration works the same way. Purple $\ce{MnO4-}$
turns colorless when it reacts, so a permanganate titration needs no indicator: the end point is the
first lasting purple tint.

## 4. Titration errors

AP lab questions ask whether a mistake makes the calculated result **too high, too low, or unchanged**.
Trace each one through the calculation, starting with the volume of titrant.

| Mistake | Effect on the titrant volume | Calculated analyte concentration |
|---|---|---|
| Buret rinsed with water, not titrant, so the titrant is diluted | More titrant needed | Too high |
| Went past the end point (too dark pink) | Volume recorded too large | Too high |
| Air bubble in the buret tip that comes out during the titration | Reading larger than what was delivered | Too high |
| Extra water added to the analyte in the flask | No change: same moles of analyte | Unchanged |
| Some analyte spilled before titrating | Less titrant needed | Too low |

<Callout type="ap-tip">
Adding water to the **flask** changes nothing, because the titration counts moles of analyte, not its
concentration in the flask. This is a favorite AP question. Water in the **buret** is different: it
dilutes the titrant, so each mL carries fewer moles than you think.
</Callout>
