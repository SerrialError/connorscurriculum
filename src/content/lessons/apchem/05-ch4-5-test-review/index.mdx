### Theory checklist

Be able to explain each of these in a sentence or two:

- Why an ionic compound dissolves: ion–dipole attractions, with the O end of water toward cations and the H end toward anions.
- Why a solution of a strong electrolyte conducts, a weak one barely conducts, and a nonelectrolyte doesn't.
- What a correct particle diagram looks like before and after a precipitate forms, including spectator ions and any leftover ions.
- Why spectator ions are left out of a net ionic equation, and why the equation must balance charge as well as atoms.
- What makes an acid strong or weak, and why weak acids stay written as molecules in a net ionic equation.
- How to spot the acid, base, and conjugate pairs in a proton transfer.
- How to tell from oxidation numbers whether a reaction is redox, and which species is oxidized.
- Why adding water to the titration flask doesn't change the result, but water in the buret does.
- Why a metal displaces another metal's ion only if it's higher on the activity series (the series is given when you need it).
- Exothermic vs. endothermic from the surroundings' point of view: the water warms when the process releases heat.
- Why heat flows until average kinetic energies, not total energies, are equal.
- Why ΔH scales with the coefficients and changes sign when the equation is reversed.
- Why breaking bonds costs energy and forming them releases it, and why bond enthalpies give only an estimate.
- Why an element in its standard state has ΔH°f = 0.

## What to memorize

The test comes with a periodic table and the
[AP Chemistry equation sheet](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-equations-sheet.pdf).
These aren't on the sheet:

| Equation | Use it for |
|---|---|
| $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ | dilution (any matching volume units) |
| $[\text{ion}] = M \times (\text{ions per formula unit})$ | ion concentrations, e.g. $[\ce{Cl-}]$ in $\ce{CaCl2}$ is $2M$ |
| $q_\text{rxn} = -q_\text{soln}$ | calorimetry: the reaction's heat is the solution's, with the opposite sign |
| $\Delta H = q_\text{rxn} / \text{mol}$ | kJ per mole of the substance the equation is written for |
| $\Delta H \approx \sum \text{broken} - \sum \text{formed}$ | ΔH estimated from bond enthalpies |
| Hess's law | reverse an equation: flip the sign of ΔH. Multiply it: multiply ΔH by the same factor |
| $\Delta E = q + w$ | first law: q > 0 for heat into the system, w > 0 for work done **on** it (compression) |

Also memorize the strong acids and bases, the solubility guidelines, and the oxidation-number rules below.

## Rules and definitions

- **Strong acids:** HCl, HBr, HI, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{HClO3}$, $\ce{HClO4}$, $\ce{H2SO4}$. Every other acid is weak. (The AP exam's list leaves out $\ce{HClO3}$.)
- **Strong bases:** group 1 hydroxides, and $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$. $\ce{NH3}$ is a weak base.
- **Always soluble:** every compound of $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{NH4+}$, or $\ce{NO3-}$. The class also covered the longer [solubility guidelines](../04-precipitation-and-net-ionic-equations/#2-which-compounds-dissolve-42): chlorides, bromides, and iodides are soluble except with $\ce{Ag+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$; sulfates except with $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$, $\ce{Hg2^2+}$, $\ce{Pb^2+}$; sulfides, carbonates, phosphates, and hydroxides are insoluble except with $\ce{NH4+}$, group 1, and (for sulfides and hydroxides) $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Sr^2+}$, $\ce{Ba^2+}$.
- **In net ionic equations**, split soluble ionic compounds, strong acids, and strong bases into ions. Keep solids, liquids, gases, weak acids, weak bases, and molecular compounds together.
- **Oxidation numbers:** an element is 0; a monatomic ion is its charge; F is −1; O is −2 (−1 in peroxides); H is +1 with nonmetals (−1 with metals); group 1 is +1 and group 2 is +2; they add up to the overall charge.
- **Oxidation** is losing electrons (oxidation number goes up). **Reduction** is gaining electrons (it goes down). Electrons lost must equal electrons gained.
- **Equivalence point:** moles of titrant exactly match the analyte by the balanced equation. The **end point** is when the indicator changes, chosen to be very close to it.
- **State function:** depends only on the start and end states, not the path. E and H are state functions; q and w are not.
- **Exothermic:** ΔH < 0, heat released, surroundings warm up. **Endothermic:** ΔH > 0, heat absorbed, surroundings cool down.
- **ΔH°f** is for forming **1 mol** of a compound from its elements in their standard states. Elements in their standard states have ΔH°f = 0.

## Common mistakes

1. Using mL instead of L in molarity, or the volume of water added instead of the solution's volume.
2. Forgetting that $\ce{CaCl2}$ gives two $\ce{Cl-}$ per formula unit.
3. Splitting a weak acid, a solid, or water into ions in a net ionic equation.
4. Skipping the mole ratio in a titration with a diprotic acid or a 5 : 1 redox ratio.
5. Dividing by only one solution's volume after mixing; use the total.
6. Using the mass of the solid instead of the mass of the whole solution in $q = mc\Delta T$.
7. Losing the sign: if the water warms up, the reaction is exothermic and its ΔH is negative.
8. Reporting ΔH in kJ for the sample instead of kJ per mole, or per mole of the wrong substance.
9. In Hess's law, flipping an equation without flipping the sign of ΔH, or multiplying the equation but not ΔH.
10. Doing reactants minus products for formation values, or products minus reactants for bond enthalpies.
11. FRQs: show each step with units, give the answer with units and sig figs, and include states in net ionic equations.
