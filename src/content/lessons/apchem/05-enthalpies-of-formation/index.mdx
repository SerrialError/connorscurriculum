## 1. Standard enthalpy of formation (5.7)

Tables can't list ΔH for every possible reaction. Instead they list one value per compound, its
**standard enthalpy of formation**, ΔH°f: the enthalpy change when **1 mole** of the compound forms from
its **elements in their standard states**.

- The **standard state** of a substance is its pure form at 1 atm (and usually 25 °C).
- The ° means standard conditions.

Each ΔH°f belongs to a **formation equation**, which always has exactly 1 mol of product and only
elements as reactants:

$$
\ce{2C(s) + 3H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> C2H5OH(l)} \qquad \Delta H^\circ_f = -277.7\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

Fractions are normal here, because the product's coefficient must be 1.

**An element in its standard state has ΔH°f = 0.** Forming $\ce{O2(g)}$ from $\ce{O2(g)}$ is no change
at all. That applies to $\ce{O2(g)}$, $\ce{H2(g)}$, $\ce{N2(g)}$, $\ce{Br2(l)}$, C(graphite), Na(s), and
Fe(s). It does **not** apply to an element in another form: $\ce{O3(g)}$, $\ce{Br2(g)}$, C(diamond), and
O(g) all have nonzero values.

<Reveal label="Worked example: is it a formation equation?">

Which of these has ΔH° equal to ΔH°f of the product?

**(a)** $\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(l)}$. Yes: 1 mol of product, from elements in their standard states.

**(b)** $\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$. No: 2 mol of product. Its ΔH° is $2 \times \Delta H^\circ_f$.

**(c)** $\ce{CO(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> CO2(g)}$. No: CO is a compound, not an element.

**(d)** $\ce{C(s) + 2Cl2(l) -> CCl4(l)}$. No: chlorine's standard state is a gas, not a liquid.

</Reveal>

Some values, in kJ/mol at 25 °C. An AP question supplies the ones it needs, so this table is for reading,
not memorizing:

| Substance | ΔH°f | Substance | ΔH°f |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2(g)}$ | −393.5 | $\ce{CH4(g)}$ | −74.8 |
| $\ce{CO(g)}$ | −110.5 | $\ce{C3H8(g)}$ | −103.8 |
| $\ce{H2O(l)}$ | −285.8 | $\ce{C2H5OH(l)}$ | −277.7 |
| $\ce{H2O(g)}$ | −241.8 | $\ce{C2H2(g)}$ | +226.7 |
| $\ce{NH3(g)}$ | −46.2 | $\ce{NO(g)}$ | +90.4 |
| $\ce{HCl(g)}$ | −92.3 | $\ce{NO2(g)}$ | +33.8 |
| $\ce{CaCO3(s)}$ | −1207.1 | $\ce{CaO(s)}$ | −635.5 |

Most compounds have negative values: they're lower in enthalpy, more stable, than their elements. A
positive value, like acetylene's, means the compound has more enthalpy than its elements.

## 2. ΔH° of a reaction from formation values (5.7, AP topic 6.8)

$$
\Delta H^\circ_\text{rxn} = \sum n\,\Delta H^\circ_f(\text{products}) - \sum m\,\Delta H^\circ_f(\text{reactants})
$$

This is on the equation sheet. n and m are the coefficients in the balanced equation.

It's Hess's law in disguise. Picture taking the reactants apart into their elements (the reverse of their
formation reactions), then building the products from those elements:

![Enthalpy diagram. Elements in their standard states at the top. An arrow from Reactants up to the elements is labeled undo formation of reactants, minus the sum of ΔH°f of the reactants. An arrow from the elements down to Products is labeled form products, plus the sum of ΔH°f of the products. A dashed arrow straight from reactants to products is ΔH°rxn. Below: ΔH°rxn = Σ ΔH°f(products) − Σ ΔH°f(reactants).](./formation-path.svg)

<Reveal label="Worked example: burning propane">

$$
\ce{C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) -> 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)}
$$

Use the table above.

**Products:** $3(-393.5) + 4(-285.8) = -1180.5 - 1143.2 = -2323.7$ kJ

**Reactants:** $(-103.8) + 5(0) = -103.8$ kJ

$$
\Delta H^\circ_\text{rxn} = -2323.7 - (-103.8) = -2219.9\ \text{kJ}
$$

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: work backward to a ΔH°f (hard)">

Ethanol burns with $\Delta H^\circ = -1367$ kJ:

$$
\ce{C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)}
$$

Use the values for $\ce{CO2(g)}$ and $\ce{H2O(l)}$ to find ΔH°f of ethanol.

$$
-1367 = [2(-393.5) + 3(-285.8)] - [\Delta H^\circ_f(\ce{C2H5OH}) + 3(0)]
$$

$$
-1367 = -1644.4 - \Delta H^\circ_f(\ce{C2H5OH})
$$

$$
\Delta H^\circ_f(\ce{C2H5OH}) = -1644.4 + 1367 = -277\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

That matches the table value of −277.7 kJ/mol to the precision of the data.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: decomposing limestone">

Find ΔH° for $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$.

$$
\Delta H^\circ = [(-635.5) + (-393.5)] - (-1207.1) = -1029.0 + 1207.1 = +178.1\ \text{kJ}
$$

Positive: making lime from limestone takes a steady supply of heat, which is why it's done in a kiln.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
The four usual mistakes:

- forgetting to multiply each ΔH°f by its **coefficient**,
- doing reactants minus products, which flips the sign,
- using $\ce{H2O(g)}$ when the equation has $\ce{H2O(l)}$, or the reverse,
- giving $\ce{O2}$ or another element a nonzero value.
</Callout>

## 3. Bond enthalpies (5.8, AP topic 6.7)

A second way to find ΔH uses **bond enthalpies**, the energy to break 1 mol of a given bond in the gas
phase. Breaking bonds takes energy and forming them releases it:

$$
\Delta H \approx \sum(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum(\text{bonds formed})
$$

The Chapter 8 lesson on bond enthalpies covers the method in detail, with a table of values. One example
here, to compare the two methods:

<Reveal label="Worked example: ammonia both ways">

$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}$. Bond enthalpies: N≡N 941, H–H 436, N–H 391 kJ/mol.

**Bonds broken:** one N≡N and three H–H: $941 + 3(436) = 2249$ kJ

**Bonds formed:** six N–H (three in each $\ce{NH3}$): $6(391) = 2346$ kJ

$$
\Delta H \approx 2249 - 2346 = -97\ \text{kJ}
$$

**From formation values:** $\Delta H^\circ = 2(-46.2) - 0 = -92.4$ kJ.

The two are close but not equal, because bond enthalpies are averages over many molecules.

</Reveal>

| | Enthalpies of formation | Bond enthalpies |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | products − reactants | broken − formed |
| Result | Accurate (from measured data) | An estimate (average values) |
| Works for | Any states, if values are listed | Gases; needs every bond drawn from Lewis structures |

<Callout type="ap-tip">
The two equations run in **opposite orders**: ΔH°f is products minus reactants, but bond enthalpy is
reactants (broken) minus products (formed). Only the ΔH°f equation is on the equation sheet, so learn
the bond-enthalpy order from its meaning: breaking costs energy (+), forming releases it (−).
</Callout>
