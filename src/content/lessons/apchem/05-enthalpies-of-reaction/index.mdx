## 1. Thermochemical equations (5.4)

A balanced equation with its enthalpy change is a **thermochemical equation**:

$$
\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -483.6\ \text{kJ}
$$

The ΔH belongs to the equation **as written**: when 2 mol of $\ce{H2}$ react with 1 mol of $\ce{O2}$ to
make 2 mol of water vapor, 483.6 kJ of heat is released. Tables often write the unit as kJ/mol, meaning
"per mole of reaction," one run of the equation with these coefficients.

## 2. Three rules (5.4)

**ΔH scales with the amount.** Enthalpy is an extensive property. Half the reaction, half the heat:

$$
\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -241.8\ \text{kJ}
$$

**Reversing a reaction flips the sign.** The same amount of energy is involved, in the other direction:

$$
\ce{2H2O(g) -> 2H2(g) + O2(g)} \qquad \Delta H = +483.6\ \text{kJ}
$$

**States matter.** Making liquid water releases more heat than making water vapor, because steam
condensing to liquid also releases heat:

$$
\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)} \qquad \Delta H = -571.6\ \text{kJ}
$$

The difference, $571.6 - 483.6 = 88.0$ kJ, is the heat released when 2 mol of water vapor condense.

## 3. Energy diagrams (AP topic 6.2)

An **energy diagram** (enthalpy diagram) draws reactants and products as levels on a vertical enthalpy
scale. The arrow from reactants to products is ΔH.

![Two enthalpy diagrams. Exothermic, methane burning: CH₄ + 2O₂ is high and CO₂ + 2H₂O(l) is lower, with a downward arrow labeled ΔH = −890 kJ. Endothermic, CaCO₃ decomposing: CaCO₃(s) is low and CaO(s) + CO₂(g) is higher, with an upward arrow labeled ΔH = +178 kJ.](./enthalpy-diagrams.svg)

- **Products lower** than reactants: energy was released. Exothermic, ΔH < 0.
- **Products higher** than reactants: energy was absorbed. Endothermic, ΔH > 0.

The same kind of diagram works for physical changes. Liquid water sits below water vapor, so condensing is
a downward (exothermic) arrow and boiling is an upward one.

<Callout type="note">
Chapter 14 adds a hump between reactants and products, the activation energy, to the same diagram. The
height of the hump controls the rate; the difference between the two ends is still ΔH.
</Callout>

## 4. Heat from an amount of reactant (5.4, AP topic 6.6)

Because ΔH scales with the amount, it works like one more mole ratio. For the equation as written,
"−890 kJ" pairs with each coefficient:

$$
\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)} \qquad \Delta H = -890\ \text{kJ}
$$

$$
\frac{890\ \text{kJ released}}{1\ \text{mol CH}_4} \qquad \frac{890\ \text{kJ released}}{2\ \text{mol O}_2}
$$

<Reveal label="Worked example: heat from burning methane">

How much heat is released when 10.0 g of $\ce{CH4}$ burns?

$\mathcal{M}(\ce{CH4}) = 12.01 + 4(1.008) = 16.04$ g/mol

$$
10.0\ \text{g} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol}}{16.04\ \text{g}} \times \frac{-890\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol CH}_4} = -555\ \text{kJ}
$$

555 kJ is released. Write $q = -555$ kJ for the reaction, or say "555 kJ released." Both are correct;
"−555 kJ released" is a double negative.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: mass from an amount of heat">

$\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +178$ kJ. What mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$ can be decomposed
by 500. kJ of heat?

$$
500.\ \text{kJ} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol CaCO}_3}{178\ \text{kJ}} = 2.81\ \text{mol}
$$

$\mathcal{M}(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09$ g/mol

$$
2.81\ \text{mol} \times 100.09\ \text{g/mol} = 281\ \text{g}
$$

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: heat with a limiting reactant (hard)">

$\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}$, $\Delta H = -571.6$ kJ. A mixture of 4.00 g $\ce{H2}$ and 16.0 g
$\ce{O2}$ reacts. How much heat is released?

- $\ce{H2}$: $4.00 / 2.016 = 1.98$ mol
- $\ce{O2}$: $16.0 / 32.00 = 0.500$ mol

0.500 mol $\ce{O2}$ needs $2 \times 0.500 = 1.00$ mol $\ce{H2}$, and 1.98 mol is available. **$\ce{O2}$
is limiting.**

$$
0.500\ \text{mol O}_2 \times \frac{571.6\ \text{kJ}}{1\ \text{mol O}_2} = 286\ \text{kJ released}
$$

The heat depends on how much reaction actually happens, so it comes from the limiting reactant.

</Reveal>

## 5. Why the temperature changes (AP topic 6.6)

In an exothermic reaction, the products have **less chemical potential energy** than the reactants
(stronger bonds formed). The difference first shows up as faster-moving product particles: higher
kinetic energy, so a higher temperature. Those particles then collide with the surroundings and pass the
energy on, until everything reaches the same temperature. An endothermic reaction does the reverse: the
products have more potential energy, the particles slow down, and heat flows in from the surroundings.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On AP free-response questions, put a sign and units on every ΔH, and keep the sign of the **heat**
straight: q for the surroundings (the water) has the opposite sign to q for the reaction. When a question
asks for heat "released," a positive number with the word "released" is fine.
</Callout>
