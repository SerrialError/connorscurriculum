## 1. Continuous and line spectra

Pass light through a prism and it spreads out into its wavelengths, a **spectrum**.

- A **continuous spectrum** contains every wavelength, like a rainbow from sunlight or a light bulb.
- A **line spectrum** contains only a few specific wavelengths, which show up as separate bright lines.

An **emission spectrum** is the light given off by gas atoms that have been excited (for example by
electricity in a neon sign). Every element's emission spectrum is a line spectrum, and each element's
pattern of lines is unique, so the spectrum works like a fingerprint for the element.

## 2. The Bohr model

Bohr explained hydrogen's line spectrum with three ideas:

1. The electron can only move in certain circular orbits, each with a fixed energy. The orbits are
   numbered $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$
2. While it stays in one allowed orbit, the electron does not give off energy.
3. The electron can jump from one orbit to another. When it does, it absorbs or emits exactly one
   photon whose energy equals the energy difference between the two levels.

- **Ground state**: $n = 1$, the lowest energy and the most stable state.
- **Excited state**: $n = 2$ or higher, with more energy and less stability.

Since only certain energy differences exist, only certain photon energies, and therefore only certain
wavelengths, can come out. That's why hydrogen gives a line spectrum rather than a continuous one.

### What was wrong with it

- It only works for **hydrogen** and other one-electron species such as $\ce{He+}$ and $\ce{Li^2+}$.
- Electrons do not really travel in fixed circular paths. The modern picture (next lesson) uses
  orbitals instead.

### What we kept

- Electrons exist only in certain energy levels.
- Energy is absorbed or emitted in fixed amounts when an electron changes levels.

<Callout type="warning">
Two of the class notes list "electrons exist only in certain levels" under the model's **limitations**.
Those two points are what the model got **right** and what carried into the modern model. The
limitations are the two bullets under "What was wrong with it."
</Callout>

## 3. Calculating the energy levels

<Callout type="not-on-ap">
The equations in this section (the Bohr energy levels and the Rydberg constant) are not on the AP
exam. They **are** on the class test, and the constant $2.18 \times 10^{-18}$ J is not given, so
memorize it.
</Callout>

The energy of the electron in level $n$ of a hydrogen atom is

$$
E_n = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)
$$

- Every level is **negative**, because the electron is held by the nucleus. Zero energy means the
  electron has been pulled all the way off ($n = \infty$).
- As $n$ gets larger, $E_n$ gets **less negative** (higher in energy), and the levels crowd closer together.

<Callout type="note">
**Grading note:** when a test question asks for the energy of a **single level** (for example
$E_3 = -2.42 \times 10^{-19}$ J), the teacher grades the magnitude and doesn't care about the sign.
For a **change** between levels, $\Delta E$, the sign does matter: negative means emitted, positive
means absorbed.
</Callout>

The energy change for a jump from an initial level $n_i$ to a final level $n_f$ is

$$
\Delta E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)
$$

| Jump | Levels | Sign of $\Delta E$ | Photon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Down (for example $n = 3 \to 2$) | $n_f$ smaller than $n_i$ | negative | **emitted** |
| Up (for example $n = 1 \to 3$) | $n_f$ larger than $n_i$ | positive | **absorbed** |

Then use the size of $\Delta E$ as the photon's energy in $E = hc/\lambda$. A wavelength is never negative.

<Reveal label="Worked example: emission from n = 6 to n = 2">

$$
\Delta E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{6^2}\right) = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}(0.2222) = -4.84 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

$\Delta E$ is negative, so a photon is **emitted**. Its wavelength:

$$
\lambda = \frac{hc}{|\Delta E|} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s})(2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})}{4.84 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}} = 4.10 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 410\ \text{nm}
$$

410 nm is violet, in the visible range. Every hydrogen line that ends at $n = 2$ is visible or near-UV.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: absorption from n = 1 to n = 3">

$$
\Delta E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\left(\frac{1}{3^2} - \frac{1}{1^2}\right) = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}(-0.8889) = +1.94 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}
$$

Positive, so the photon is **absorbed**.
$\lambda = hc/\Delta E = 1.03 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 103\ \text{nm}$, in the ultraviolet.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: work backward to the starting level">

A hydrogen line at 434 nm ends in $n = 2$. Which level did the electron start in?

Photon energy: $E = hc/\lambda = (6.626 \times 10^{-34})(2.998 \times 10^{8}) / (434 \times 10^{-9}) = 4.58 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
The electron dropped, so $\Delta E = -4.58 \times 10^{-19}$ J:

$$
-4.58 \times 10^{-19} = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{n_i^2} = 0.210
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{n_i^2} = 0.040
\quad\Rightarrow\quad n_i = 5
$$

</Reveal>

### Which jump gives the most energy?

The levels are farthest apart near $n = 1$, so:

- Any drop **to** $n = 1$ releases more energy (shorter wavelength) than any drop that ends at $n = 2$ or higher.
- Between two drops, the bigger energy gap gives the **higher frequency** and **shorter wavelength** photon.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
The AP version of this is qualitative (topic 3.12): a photon's energy equals the gap between two
electron energy levels, so a bigger gap means a higher-frequency, shorter-wavelength photon. You
won't need the Rydberg constant on the AP exam.
</Callout>
