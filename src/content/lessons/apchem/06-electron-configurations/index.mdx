An **electron configuration** lists which subshells an atom's electrons are in, for example
$1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4$ for oxygen. The big number is $n$, the letter is the subshell, and the superscript
is the number of electrons in that subshell.

## 1. The three filling rules

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Aufbau principle** | Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first. |
| **Pauli exclusion principle** | No two electrons in the same atom have the same four quantum numbers. So one orbital holds at most **two** electrons, and they must have **opposite spins** (↑↓). |
| **Hund's rule** | In a set of equal-energy orbitals (like the three 2p), put **one** electron in each, all with the same spin, before pairing any up. |

### Filling order

$$
1s \to 2s \to 2p \to 3s \to 3p \to 4s \to 3d \to 4p \to 5s \to 4d \to 5p \to 6s \to 4f \to 5d \to 6p \to 7s \to 5f
$$

You don't need to memorize this list. Read it off the periodic table, left to right and top to bottom:

- Groups 1–2 are the **s block**.
- Groups 13–18 are the **p block**.
- The transition metals are the **d block**. The d subshell is one shell behind the period: period 4 fills **3d**.
- The two rows at the bottom are the **f block**, two shells behind: period 6 fills **4f**.

## 2. Orbital diagrams

Each orbital is a box or line, and each electron is an arrow. Here is nitrogen ($Z = 7$), $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$:

| 1s | 2s | 2p | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ↑↓ | ↑↓ | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |

Three electrons in 2p go into **separate** orbitals with the same spin (Hund's rule), so nitrogen has
**3 unpaired electrons**.

| 2p diagram for nitrogen | Verdict |
|---|---|
| ↑ ↑ ↑ | Correct |
| ↑↓ ↑ \_\_ | Breaks **Hund's rule** (paired too early) |
| ↑↑ ↑ \_\_ | Breaks the **Pauli principle** (two electrons with the same spin in one orbital) |

<Callout type="warning">
Count unpaired electrons from the **orbital diagram**, not from the superscript. Sulfur's $3p^4$ is
↑↓ ↑ ↑, so it has 2 unpaired electrons, not 4.
</Callout>

## 3. Noble-gas (condensed) configurations

Replace the filled inner shells with the symbol of the noble gas before the element, in brackets.

| Element | Full configuration | Noble-gas form | Unpaired |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na (11) | $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^1$ | $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^1$ | 1 |
| P (15) | $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^3$ | $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^3$ | 3 |
| Ti (22) | $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^2$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^2$ | 2 |
| Br (35) | $\ldots 3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^5$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^5$ | 1 |

The electrons written after the bracket in the outermost shell (highest $n$) are the **valence
electrons**. The bracketed noble-gas core is the **core electrons**. For main-group elements, the
number of valence electrons matches the group: 1 for group 1, 2 for group 2, 3 for group 13, up to 8
for group 18. Bromine has 7 valence electrons ($4s^2\,4p^5$); its filled $3d^{10}$ doesn't count.

<Callout type="note">
Check your work: for a neutral atom the superscripts must add up to the atomic number.
</Callout>

## 4. Exceptions: Cr and Cu

<Callout type="not-on-ap">
The AP exam doesn't test the exceptions to the aufbau principle. Your teacher may, so learn these two.
</Callout>

| Element | Expected | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Cr (24) | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^4$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^1\,3d^5$ |
| Cu (29) | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^9$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^1\,3d^{10}$ |

Moving one 4s electron into 3d gives a **half-filled** ($d^5$) or **completely filled** ($d^{10}$) d
subshell, which is extra stable. Mo and Ag, directly below Cr and Cu, do the same thing. Cr ends up
with 6 unpaired electrons and Cu with 1.

## 5. Configurations of ions

- **Anions** gain electrons into the next open spot: $\ce{S}$ is $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^4$, so $\ce{S^2-}$ is $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^6 = [\ce{Ar}]$.
- **Main-group cations** lose their valence electrons and usually end up like a noble gas: $\ce{Mg^2+}$ is $[\ce{Ne}]$.
- **Transition-metal cations lose their 4s electrons first**, before any 3d electrons, even though 4s filled first.

| Atom or ion | Configuration |
|---|---|
| Fe | $[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^6$ |
| $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^6$ |
| $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^5$ |
| $\ce{Zn^2+}$ | $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}$ |

Atoms or ions with the same configuration are **isoelectronic**. $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{F-}$, Ne,
$\ce{Na+}$, and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ all have 10 electrons and the configuration $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6$.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On the AP exam (topic 1.5), configurations come with a reason: **Coulomb's law**. The attraction
between an electron and the nucleus grows with the nuclear charge and shrinks with distance. Electrons
in a higher shell are farther away and less attracted, so they're easier to remove. Expect to explain
answers this way in Chapter 7.
</Callout>
