Chapter 6 starts with light, because light is the tool chemists use to work out where an atom's
electrons are. Everything here comes down to two equations and the habit of converting units first.

<Callout type="warning">
On the class test you get a periodic table but **no constants**. Memorize these four:

- Planck's constant: $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$
- Speed of light: $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$
- Rydberg (Bohr) constant: $2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$ (used in the next lesson)
- Avogadro's number: $6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$

The AP exam does print $h$, $c$, and Avogadro's number on its equation sheet.
</Callout>

## 1. Light as a wave

**Electromagnetic radiation** is energy that travels as a wave and moves through a vacuum at the speed
of light, $c$. Every kind, from radio waves to gamma rays, moves at the same speed. The kinds differ
only in wavelength and frequency.

- **Wavelength** ($\lambda$, lambda): the distance from one crest to the next, in meters.
- **Frequency** ($\nu$, nu): how many crests pass a point each second, in $\text{s}^{-1}$, also called hertz (Hz).

$$
c = \lambda \nu
$$

Because $c$ is fixed, wavelength and frequency are **inversely** related: a shorter wave has a higher frequency.

### Units you need

| Unit | In meters or hertz |
|---|---|
| 1 nm (nanometer) | $10^{-9}\ \text{m}$ |
| 1 µm (micrometer) | $10^{-6}\ \text{m}$ |
| 1 Å (angstrom) | $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ |
| 1 MHz (megahertz) | $10^{6}\ \text{Hz} = 10^{6}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ |
| 1 GHz (gigahertz) | $10^{9}\ \text{Hz}$ |

<Callout type="warning">
Convert nm to m **before** you use $c = \lambda\nu$ or $E = hc/\lambda$. Most wrong answers on these
problems are off by exactly $10^{9}$.
</Callout>

### The electromagnetic spectrum

From **shortest wavelength (highest energy)** to **longest wavelength (lowest energy)**:

**gamma rays → X-rays → ultraviolet → visible → infrared → microwaves → radio waves**

Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to about 750 nm (red). A way to remember the visible
order is ROYGBIV, where red has the longest wavelength and violet the shortest.

<Reveal label="Worked example: wavelength of a radio station">

An FM station broadcasts at 88.7 MHz. What is the wavelength?

$$
\lambda = \frac{c}{\nu} = \frac{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}{88.7 \times 10^{6}\ \text{s}^{-1}} = 3.38\ \text{m}
$$

That's a few meters, which fits: radio waves are the longest waves on the spectrum.

</Reveal>

Two more vocabulary words from class: **monochromatic** radiation has a single wavelength (a laser),
and **polychromatic** radiation has more than one (sunlight, a light bulb).

## 2. Energy comes in packets

Around 1900 two observations didn't fit the idea of light as a smooth wave:

1. **Blackbody radiation.** A hot solid glows, first red and then white as it gets hotter. Planck could
   only explain the colors by assuming energy is given off in fixed amounts, called **quanta**
   (one amount is a **quantum**).
2. **The photoelectric effect.** Light shining on a clean metal surface knocks electrons out of it.
   Einstein explained this by treating light as a stream of energy packets, called **photons**.

The energy of **one** photon is

$$
E = h\nu \qquad\text{or, using } c = \lambda\nu, \qquad E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

So energy goes **up** with frequency and **down** with wavelength. A violet photon carries more energy
than a red one, and an X-ray photon carries far more than either.

<Callout type="note">
**Quantum theory** says energy is quantized: it can only take certain allowed values, never anything
in between. Your class notes flag "quanta" and "photon" as words that may appear on the test.
</Callout>

<Reveal label="Worked example: one photon and a mole of photons">

A violet laser pointer emits light at 405 nm. Find the frequency, the energy of one photon, and the
energy of one mole of photons.

**Frequency:**
$$
\nu = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}{405 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m}} = 7.40 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}
$$

**One photon:**
$$
E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s})(7.40 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 4.90 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

**One mole of photons:**
$$
(4.90 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J})(6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}) = 2.95 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/mol} = 295\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

</Reveal>

### The photoelectric effect in detail

Each metal needs a minimum amount of energy to pull one electron off its surface. This minimum is
called the **work function**.

- One photon gives its energy to one electron. If that photon's energy is **below** the minimum, no
  electron leaves, **no matter how bright the light is**. Brighter light means more photons, not
  stronger ones.
- If the photon's energy is **above** the minimum, an electron is ejected, and the leftover energy
  becomes the electron's kinetic energy:

$$
KE_{\text{electron}} = E_{\text{photon}} - E_{\text{minimum}}
$$

- Above the threshold, brighter light ejects **more** electrons, but each one has the same kinetic energy.

<Reveal label="Worked example: does the light eject electrons?">

A metal needs at least $4.00 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ to remove an electron. Will 600. nm light
eject electrons? Will 400. nm light?

$$
E_{600} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(2.998 \times 10^{8})}{600. \times 10^{-9}} = 3.31 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

That's below $4.00 \times 10^{-19}$ J, so **no electrons** come off, however bright the 600 nm light is.

$$
E_{400} = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(2.998 \times 10^{8})}{400. \times 10^{-9}} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

That's enough. Each ejected electron has
$KE = 4.97 \times 10^{-19} - 4.00 \times 10^{-19} = 9.7 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}$.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="note">
One classmate's notes cross out the work function and blackbody radiation, but another study guide
for this test includes photoelectric kinetic-energy problems. Know the idea and the subtraction above
in case they come up.
</Callout>

## 3. What different kinds of light do to molecules

This part is AP material (topic 3.11). Each region of the spectrum carries the right amount of energy
to cause a different change in a molecule:

| Radiation | What absorbing it does |
|---|---|
| Microwave | Makes molecules **rotate** faster |
| Infrared | Makes bonds **vibrate** |
| Ultraviolet and visible | Moves **electrons** to higher energy levels |

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On the AP exam, $E = h\nu$ and $c = \lambda\nu$ are on the equation sheet. What they test is the
reasoning: which photon has more energy, how wavelength and frequency are related, and which region of
the spectrum causes which kind of change in a molecule.
</Callout>
