## 6.1: The Wave Nature of Light

- **Electromagnetic radiation** (also known as radiant energy)
  - Types: visible light, radio waves, infrared radiation (heat), UV radiation, X-rays
  - All move at the same **speed of light** $= 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
- **Wavelength**: the distance between identical points on successive waves (such as two adjacent peaks or troughs)
  - Symbol: $\lambda$
  - Usually given in nanometers: $1 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 1\ \text{nm}$
- **Frequency**: the number of times per second that one complete wavelength passes a given point
  - Symbol: $\nu$
  - Given in $\text{s}^{-1}$ (or Hz)
- Wavelength and frequency have an **inverse relationship**.
- Equation: $c = \lambda\nu$

## 6.2: Quantized Energy and Photons

- **Blackbody radiation**: emission of light from hot objects
- **Photoelectric effect**: emission of electrons from metal surfaces on which light shines (a minimum frequency of light, different for different metals, is required for e⁻ to be emitted)
- **Emission spectra**: emission of light from electronically excited gas atoms

<Callout type="not-on-ap">
Blackbody radiation isn't on the AP exam. But the idea behind the other two, that a photon's energy
($E = h\nu$) has to match an energy difference in the atom, **is** on the AP exam (topic 3.12). So
know emission spectra and the photoelectric effect for both tests.
</Callout>

- **Max Planck** proposed that energy can only be released or absorbed by atoms in discrete "chunks" of a minimum size.
  - He gave the name **"quantum"** (meaning "fixed amount") to the smallest quantity of energy that can be emitted or absorbed as electromagnetic radiation.
  - Matter can emit and absorb energy only in **whole-number** multiples of $h\nu$.
- **Equations** (calculated for **one photon**; multiply by Avogadro's number to get the answer for a mole of photons):

$$
E = h\nu \qquad E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

- $E$ is the energy (J), and $h$ is Planck's constant, $6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s.
- $h$ and $c$ are constants, so $E$ and $\lambda$ have an inverse relationship.

**Photoelectric effect drawing:** light shines into a glass jar onto a metal plate, and electrons fly
off the metal toward a rod.

- **Einstein** assumed that radiant energy striking a metal surface behaves like a stream of tiny energy packets.
  - Each packet (a "particle" of energy) is a **photon**.
  - The energy of a photon is $E = h\nu$.
- **Work function**: the amount of energy electrons need to overcome the attractive forces holding them in the metal.
  - If the photons striking the metal have less energy than the work function, electrons can't get enough energy to escape the metal.

## 6.3: Line Spectra and the Bohr Model

- The **Bohr model** only works for atoms with one e⁻ (hydrogen).
- Equations:

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
\qquad (n_2 > n_1,\ \text{both positive whole numbers})
$$

- Rydberg's constant $R_H = 1.096776 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}$

$$
E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)
$$

The energy of an e⁻ sitting in a ring (not jumping).

$$
\Delta E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)
$$

The energy associated with an e⁻ jumping. The constant $2.18 \times 10^{-18}$ J is Planck's constant × the speed of light × Rydberg's constant.

These two are opposites:
- When $n_f > n_i$: **absorption** ($\Delta E$ positive, energy absorbed)
- When $n_f < n_i$: **emission** ($\Delta E$ negative, energy released)

- **Ground state**: most stable, lowest energy ($n = 1$)
- **Excited state**: not the lowest, less stable ($n = 2, 3, 4, \ldots$)
- $n$ is called the **principal quantum number**.

## 6.5: Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Orbitals

| Quantum number | Name | Values |
|---|---|---|
| $n$ | principal energy level (the period) | 1, 2, 3, … |
| $l$ | sublevel: 0 = s, 1 = p, 2 = d, 3 = f | 0 to $n - 1$ |
| $m_l$ | orbitals | $-l$ to $+l$ |
| $m_s$ | spin | $+\tfrac{1}{2}$, $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ |

One orbital holds two electrons (↑↓), one with each spin.

**Energy levels in the hydrogen atom** (in place of the textbook figure). In hydrogen, every subshell
in the same shell has the **same** energy:

| Shell | Subshells (all the same energy) | Orbitals |
|---|---|---|
| $n = 1$ | 1s | 1 |
| $n = 2$ | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 = 4 |
| $n = 3$ | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 |

## 6.7: Many-Electron Atoms

In a many-electron atom, subshells in the same shell have **different** energy levels. In hydrogen
they have the same energy (see 6.5). Energy goes up in this order (in place of the textbook figure):

$$
1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p
$$

- In the same shell: $ns < np < nd < nf$.
- 4p is the highest energy shown. 3p and 3d are **not** the same energy.
- Orbitals in the same subshell (for example, the three 3p orbitals) are degenerate: they have the same energy.

**Rules:**
- **Pauli exclusion principle**: no two e⁻ in a given atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers ($n$, $l$, $m_l$, $m_s$).
- **Aufbau principle**: electrons fill the atomic orbitals of the lowest available energy levels first.
- **Hund's rule**: put one electron with the same spin on each line before going back for the other way: ↑↓ ↑ ↑
