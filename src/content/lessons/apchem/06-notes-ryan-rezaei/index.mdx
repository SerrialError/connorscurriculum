## Equations

| Equation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $E = h\nu$ | energy of 1 photon; $h$ is Planck's constant, $6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$ |
| $c = \lambda\nu$ | speed of light $=$ wavelength $\times$ frequency; $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ |
| $E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$ | energy of 1 photon from its wavelength |

- **Direct relationship**: as $\nu$ goes up, $E$ goes up.
- **Indirect relationship**: as $\nu$ goes up, $\lambda$ goes down.
- $\nu$ is in Hz or $\text{s}^{-1}$.

**Units:**
- $1\ \text{m} = 1 \times 10^{9}\ \text{nm}$ and $1\ \text{m} = 1 \times 10^{6}\ \text{µm}$
- M (mega) $= 10^{6}$, so $1\ \text{MHz} = 1 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Hz}$
- $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2$ and $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2$

**Spectrum:**
- Radio waves have the longest $\lambda$.
- Gamma rays have the shortest $\lambda$.

## Observations

1. ~~**Blackbody radiation**: emission of light from hot objects.~~ *(Crossed out in Ryan's notes.)* Objects appear black before heating.
2. **Photoelectric effect**: emission of e⁻ from metal surfaces on which light shines (based on the photon's specific energy).
3. **Emission spectra**: emission of light from electronically excited gas atoms.
   - Energy is added to the atom → e⁻ jumps to an excited state → energy is released when it goes back to the ground state.

\* When solids are heated, they emit radiation (the red glow hot objects produce).

## Definitions

- **Electromagnetic radiation**: a form of energy that has wave characteristics and propagates through a vacuum at $2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$.
- **Photon**: the smallest increment (a quantum) of radiant energy.
- ~~**Work function**: the minimum amount of energy required to remove an e⁻ from the surface of a metal.~~ *(Crossed out in Ryan's notes.)*
  - Below the minimum energy: no e⁻ emitted from the surface.
  - Above the minimum energy: the photoelectric effect occurs. The e⁻ is removed, and the photon's excess energy is converted to kinetic energy.
- **Monochromatic**: radiation made of 1 wavelength.
- **Polychromatic**: radiation containing 2 or more wavelengths.

## Bohr's model (only for the H atom)

- $n = 1$ (ground state): lowest energy, most stable.
- $n = 2$ and up (excited state): higher energy, less stable.

$$
E = 2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)
\qquad
\Delta E = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)
$$

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** the energy-level equation needs a minus sign:
$E_n = -2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)$. Bound electrons always have
negative energy. On the test, the teacher grades only the magnitude of a single level's energy, but
the sign of $\Delta E$ (emitted vs. absorbed) does count.
</Callout>

- $\Delta E > 0$, $n_f > n_i$: photons **absorbed**. ($\Delta E$ is positive for absorbance.)
- $\Delta E < 0$, $n_f < n_i$: photons **emitted**. ($\Delta E$ is negative for emission.)

**Bohr's model:**
1. e⁻ travel in specific $n$ orbits.
2. An e⁻ in a stable orbit doesn't radiate energy.
3. An e⁻ can jump from one energy level to another.

**Limitations of Bohr's model:**
1. Only works for the H atom.
2. e⁻ only exist in certain, specific energy levels.
3. A discrete quantity of energy is either absorbed or emitted when an e⁻ transitions from one level to another.

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** only #1 is a real limitation. #2 and #3 are things the Bohr model got **right**, and
they carried over into the modern model. The other main limitation is that electrons don't actually
move in fixed circular orbits.
</Callout>

## Quantum numbers

| Symbol | Name | Describes | Values |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | principal quantum number | orbit size / energy level; matches the period | 1, 2, 3, … |
| $l$ | azimuthal quantum number | shape / subshell | 0 to $n - 1$ |
| $m_l$ | magnetic quantum number | how the orbital is oriented | $-l$ to $+l$ |
| $m_s$ | spin quantum number | spin of the e⁻ | $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}$ |

| $l$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subshell | s (sphere) | p (four-leaf drawing) | d (clover) | f |
| Max e⁻ | 2 | 6 | 10 | 14 |

- $n^2$ = number of orbitals
- $2n^2$ = maximum number of e⁻

**Example: $4d^7$**
- d → $l = 2$, and $n = 4$, with 7 e⁻.
- $4^2 = 16$ orbitals in the $n = 4$ shell, and $2(4)^2 = 32$ e⁻ max.

## Orbital energies

The 2s and 2p of an atom with 1 e⁻ ($\ce{He+}$ or H) have the same energy. But for multi-electron
atoms, 2s is lower in energy.

## Filling rules

- **Hund's rule**: put 1 e⁻ in each orbital before going back and giving another: ↑ ↑ ↑ → ↑↓ ↑↓ ↑↓
- **Aufbau principle**: e⁻ fill up from lowest energy to highest energy: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6 \ldots$
- **Pauli's exclusion principle**: no 2 e⁻ in the same atom can have the exact same set of all 4 quantum numbers ($n$, $l$, $m_l$, $m_s$).
