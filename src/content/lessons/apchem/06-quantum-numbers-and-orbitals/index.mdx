## 1. From orbits to orbitals

**Matter behaves like a wave, too (6.4).** Light can act like a particle (photons), and de Broglie
proposed the reverse: anything that moves has a wavelength, $\lambda = h/mv$. Because $h$ is so tiny,
the wavelength only matters for particles with a very small mass, like electrons. A thrown baseball's
wavelength is far too small to ever notice. Electron beams really do diffract like waves, which
confirmed the idea.

The **uncertainty principle** (Heisenberg) says we can't know both the exact location and the exact
speed (momentum) of an electron at the same time. So instead of a path like Bohr's orbit, quantum
mechanics gives an **orbital**: a region of space where the electron is likely to be found.

- The square of the wave function gives the **probability density** (also called electron density):
  how likely the electron is to be found at each point. Orbital drawings show where that density is high.
- A **node** is a place where the probability is zero. Higher-energy orbitals have more nodes. For
  example, 2s has one spherical node and 3s has two.

## 2. The four quantum numbers

Each electron in an atom is described by four quantum numbers.

| Symbol | Name | What it tells you | Allowed values |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | principal | size and energy (the shell; matches the period) | $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ |
| $l$ | azimuthal (angular momentum) | shape (the subshell) | $0$ up to $n - 1$ |
| $m_l$ | magnetic | which way the orbital points | $-l$ to $+l$, whole numbers |
| $m_s$ | spin | the electron's spin | $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ or $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ |

Each value of $l$ has a letter:

| $l$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Subshell | s | p | d | f |
| Orbitals ($2l + 1$) | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 |
| Max electrons | 2 | 6 | 10 | 14 |

### Walking through the shells

| $n$ | $l$ values | Subshells | $m_l$ values | Orbitals ($n^2$) | Max electrons ($2n^2$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | 1s | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 0, 1 | 2s, 2p | 0; −1, 0, +1 | 4 | 8 |
| 3 | 0, 1, 2 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 0; −1 to +1; −2 to +2 | 9 | 18 |
| 4 | 0, 1, 2, 3 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | … | 16 | 32 |

<Callout type="warning">
$n^2$ counts **orbitals**. $2n^2$ counts **electrons**. And since $l$ stops at $n - 1$, there is no
1p, 2d, or 3f subshell.
</Callout>

<Reveal label="Worked example: which sets are allowed?">

Check each set $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$:

- $(3, 2, -2, +\tfrac{1}{2})$: $l = 2$ is allowed for $n = 3$, and $m_l = -2$ is within $-2$ to $+2$. **Allowed** (a 3d electron).
- $(2, 2, 0, +\tfrac{1}{2})$: $l$ can only be 0 or 1 when $n = 2$. **Not allowed.**
- $(4, 1, -2, -\tfrac{1}{2})$: for $l = 1$, $m_l$ can only be $-1, 0, +1$. **Not allowed.**
- $(1, 0, 0, 0)$: $m_s$ must be $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}$. **Not allowed.**

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: describe 4d⁷">

- $n = 4$, and d means $l = 2$.
- $m_l$ runs from $-2$ to $+2$, so the 4d subshell has 5 orbitals.
- Seven electrons in five orbitals, filled by Hund's rule: ↑↓ ↑↓ ↑ ↑ ↑, which leaves **3 unpaired**.
- The whole $n = 4$ shell has $4^2 = 16$ orbitals and can hold $2(4)^2 = 32$ electrons.

</Reveal>

## 3. Orbital shapes

- **s orbitals** are spheres. Each shell has one. A 2s orbital is bigger than a 1s, and a 3s is bigger still.
- **p orbitals** have two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus (a dumbbell or "propeller"). Starting at
  $n = 2$, each shell has three of them, $p_x$, $p_y$, and $p_z$, at right angles to each other.
  A p orbital has a **node** (zero chance of finding the electron) at the nucleus.
- **d orbitals** mostly have four lobes (a cloverleaf, or the "double-sided pacifier" from class).
  Starting at $n = 3$, each shell has five.

A higher $n$ means a bigger orbital, so the electron is farther from the nucleus on average.

## 4. Orbital energies in many-electron atoms

- In **hydrogen** (one electron), energy depends only on $n$: 2s and 2p have the same energy.
  Orbitals with the same energy are called **degenerate**.
- In atoms with **two or more electrons**, the electrons repel and shield each other, and energy
  depends on both $n$ and $l$: **2s is lower than 2p**, and $3s < 3p < 3d$.
- The reason: an s electron spends some time very close to the nucleus (it **penetrates** the inner
  electrons), so it feels a larger effective nuclear charge than a p electron in the same shell.
- This is also why 4s fills before 3d, which you'll use in the next lesson.

<Reveal label="Check yourself: in which species are 2s and 2p the same energy?">

Only one-electron species: H, $\ce{He+}$, $\ce{Li^2+}$. Even $\ce{He}$ and $\ce{Li+}$ have two
electrons, so their 2s is lower than their 2p.

</Reveal>
