Every periodic trend in this chapter is explained by two things: **how strongly the nucleus pulls on
the outer electrons** (effective nuclear charge) and **how far away those electrons are** (which shell
they're in).

## 1. What Zeff is

An outer electron doesn't feel the full charge of the nucleus. The electrons between it and the
nucleus block, or **shield**, part of that charge. The net positive charge an electron actually feels
is the **effective nuclear charge**:

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S
$$

- $Z$ is the atomic number, the number of protons.
- $S$ is the **shielding (screening) constant**, roughly the number of electrons between the nucleus
  and the electron you're looking at.

Your class notes also write Zeff as **ENC**.

## 2. The quick estimate

Treat every core electron as blocking one full proton and every valence electron as blocking nothing:

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - (\text{number of core electrons}) = \text{number of valence electrons}
$$

| Atom | $Z$ | Core electrons | Quick $Z_{\text{eff}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | 11 | 10 | 1 |
| Mg | 12 | 10 | 2 |
| Cl | 17 | 10 | 7 |
| K | 19 | 18 | 1 |

The quick method is enough for deciding which way a trend goes ("just look at the valence
electrons"). For an actual number, use Slater's rules.

## 3. Slater's rules

<Callout type="not-on-ap">
Slater's rules are not on the AP exam, which only uses Zeff to explain trends. They are on the class
test.
</Callout>

To find $Z_{\text{eff}}$ for an electron in shell $n$ (an s or p electron):

1. Write the configuration and group it by shell: $(1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p)\ldots$
2. Ignore everything in higher shells than your electron, and **don't count the electron itself**.
3. Add up the shielding:

| Other electrons in… | Each one adds to $S$ |
|---|---|
| the same shell $n$ | 0.35 (but 0.30 for the other electron in 1s) |
| shell $n - 1$ | 0.85 |
| shell $n - 2$ or lower | 1.00 |

4. Subtract: $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$.

<Reveal label="Worked example: Zeff for a 3p electron in chlorine">

Cl ($Z = 17$): $1s^2\ (2s^2 2p^6)\ (3s^2 3p^5)$

- Same shell ($n = 3$): 7 electrons, minus the one we're looking at, is **6**. $6 \times 0.35 = 2.10$
- Shell $n - 1$ ($n = 2$): **8** electrons. $8 \times 0.85 = 6.80$
- Shell $n - 2$ ($n = 1$): **2** electrons. $2 \times 1.00 = 2.00$

$S = 2.10 + 6.80 + 2.00 = 10.90$, so $Z_{\text{eff}} = 17 - 10.90 = 6.10$.

</Reveal>

<Reveal label="Worked example: Zeff for the 4s electron in potassium">

K ($Z = 19$): $1s^2\ (2s^2 2p^6)\ (3s^2 3p^6)\ (4s^1)$

- Same shell ($n = 4$): no other electrons. $0$
- Shell $n - 1$ ($n = 3$): **8** electrons. $8 \times 0.85 = 6.80$
- Shell $n - 2$ and lower ($n = 1$ and $2$): **10** electrons. $10 \times 1.00 = 10.00$

$S = 16.80$, so $Z_{\text{eff}} = 19 - 16.80 = 2.20$.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
One set of class notes gets **2.02** for potassium. The setup in those notes is right, but the
arithmetic is off: $19 - (2 + 8 + 6.80) = 19 - 16.80 = 2.20$. Also note that the $n - 2$ group
covers **both** the first and second shells (10 electrons), not just 2s and 2p.
</Callout>

| Electron | $S$ | $Z_{\text{eff}}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Li 2s | $2(0.85) = 1.70$ | 1.30 |
| C 2p | $3(0.35) + 2(0.85) = 2.75$ | 3.25 |
| N 2p | $4(0.35) + 2(0.85) = 3.10$ | 3.90 |
| F 2p | $6(0.35) + 2(0.85) = 3.80$ | 5.20 |
| Na 3s | $8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 8.80$ | 2.20 |
| Mg 3s | $1(0.35) + 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 9.15$ | 2.85 |
| S 3p | $5(0.35) + 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 10.55$ | 5.45 |

## 4. How Zeff changes

- **Across a period (left to right), Zeff increases.** Each step adds one proton, but the new electron
  goes into the same shell, and electrons in the same shell shield each other poorly (only 0.35). Most
  of the added charge isn't canceled.
- **Down a group, Zeff on the valence electrons barely changes.** Na and K both come out at 2.20.
  What changes going down is the **distance**: each period adds a shell, so the valence electrons sit
  farther from the nucleus.
- **Core electrons feel a much larger Zeff** than valence electrons, because fewer electrons are
  between them and the nucleus.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On the AP exam, justify trends with **Coulomb's law**: more protons in the same shell means a
stronger pull; an electron in a higher shell is farther away and feels a weaker pull. Say "greater
effective nuclear charge" or "more protons with the same shielding." Don't just say "it's to the right."
</Callout>
