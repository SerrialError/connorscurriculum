## 1. Building the periodic table (7.1)

- **Mendeleev** (1869) arranged the known elements in order of **atomic mass**, grouping elements with
  similar properties in the same column. He left **gaps** for elements nobody had found yet and
  predicted their properties. When gallium and germanium were discovered, they matched his predictions.
- A few elements end up out of order by mass (tellurium is heavier than iodine, but iodine clearly
  belongs with the halogens).
- **Moseley** (1913) used X-rays to show that each element has a different **atomic number**, the
  number of protons. Ordering by atomic number fixes the out-of-order pairs, and that's how the modern
  table is arranged.

## 2. Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids (7.6)

| | Metals | Nonmetals | Metalloids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where | left and middle | upper right | along the staircase (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) |
| Look and feel | shiny, malleable, ductile | dull; solids are brittle | in between |
| Conduct heat and electricity | well | poorly | somewhat (semiconductors, like Si) |
| Ionization energy | low | high | in between |
| Ions formed | cations | anions (with metals) | rarely ions |
| Oxides | **basic** ionic solids | **acidic** molecular oxides | in between |

**Metallic character** increases down a group and to the left, the opposite direction from ionization
energy. The lower the IE, the more easily the atom gives up electrons and the more "metal-like" it is.

### Oxides as acids and bases

- A **metal oxide** dissolved in water makes a metal **hydroxide** (a base):
  $\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}$. Metal oxides also react with acids to make a salt and water:
  $\ce{MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}$.
- A **nonmetal oxide** dissolved in water makes an **acid**:
  $\ce{CO2(g) + H2O(l) -> H2CO3(aq)}$ and $\ce{SO3(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO4(aq)}$. Nonmetal oxides
  react with bases to make a salt and water: $\ce{CO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)}$.

<Callout type="note">
Shortcut: **metal oxide = base, nonmetal oxide = acid.** Acid rain comes from nonmetal oxides like
$\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{NO2}$ dissolving in rainwater.
</Callout>

## 3. Group 1 and Group 2 metals (7.7)

### Alkali metals (group 1: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs)

- Soft (they can be cut with a knife), shiny, and with low melting points and low densities.
- One valence electron ($ns^1$) and the **lowest ionization energies** in their periods, so they lose
  that electron easily and form $+1$ ions.
- They're so reactive they're never found free in nature, and they're stored under oil.
- They react with water to make **hydrogen gas** and a **hydroxide**:

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

- **Reactivity increases going down the group** (Li → Cs), because IE gets lower as the valence
  electron gets farther from the nucleus. Potassium reacts with water more violently than sodium.
- They react with nonmetals to form ionic compounds: $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$.
- Their compounds give **flame colors**: lithium red, sodium yellow-orange, potassium violet.

### Alkaline earth metals (group 2: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba)

- Harder, denser, and higher-melting than the alkali metals.
- Two valence electrons ($ns^2$), so they form $+2$ ions. Their first IE is higher than group 1's, so
  they're **less reactive** than the alkali metal next to them.
- Reactivity again **increases down the group**. Be doesn't react with water, Mg reacts only slowly
  (faster with steam), and Ca and the ones below it react readily:

$$
\ce{Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

## 4. Selected nonmetals (7.8)

### Hydrogen

Hydrogen sits above group 1 because of its $1s^1$ configuration, but it's a **nonmetal**. Its IE is far
higher than the alkali metals'. It usually forms molecular compounds by sharing electrons (like
$\ce{H2O}$), and with very active metals it can gain an electron to form the **hydride** ion,
$\ce{H-}$ (as in $\ce{NaH}$).

### Group 16: oxygen

- Oxygen exists as $\ce{O2}$ and as **ozone**, $\ce{O3}$. Two forms of the same element are called **allotropes**.
- Oxygen is very electronegative (second only to fluorine). It usually forms oxides containing $\ce{O^2-}$,
  but peroxides ($\ce{O2^2-}$, as in $\ce{H2O2}$) also exist.
- Going down group 16 (O, S, Se, Te, Po), metallic character increases. Sulfur commonly exists as $\ce{S8}$ rings.

### Group 17: the halogens

- They exist as **diatomic molecules**: $\ce{F2}$ and $\ce{Cl2}$ are gases, $\ce{Br2}$ is a liquid, and $\ce{I2}$ is a solid at room temperature.
- With seven valence electrons and very negative electron affinities, they gain one electron to form $-1$ ions (halides).
- **Reactivity decreases going down the group.** Fluorine is the most reactive element of all.
- A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ion of a less reactive one:

$$
\ce{Cl2(g) + 2NaBr(aq) -> 2NaCl(aq) + Br2(aq)}
$$

The reverse, $\ce{Br2}$ with $\ce{NaCl}$, doesn't happen.

### Group 18: the noble gases

- Monatomic gases with filled $ns^2\,np^6$ shells (He is $1s^2$). They have very high IEs and positive
  electron affinities, so they're very unreactive.
- They aren't completely inert, though. The heavier ones have lower IEs, and xenon forms compounds with
  the most electronegative elements, such as $\ce{XeF2}$, $\ce{XeF4}$, and $\ce{XeO3}$. Krypton forms
  $\ce{KrF2}$. No compounds of He, Ne, or Ar are known under normal conditions.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
Every group trend here comes back to the Chapter 7 ideas: metals get **more** reactive down a group
because IE drops, and nonmetals get **less** reactive down a group because Zeff stays about the same
while the atoms get bigger, so they attract an extra electron less strongly.
</Callout>
