## Effective nuclear charge

**Zeff (effective nuclear charge)**: the net positive charge experienced by a specific e⁻ in a
multi-electron atom.

$$
Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S \qquad (Z = \text{number of protons})
$$

Slater's shielding values:

| Electrons in… | Each counts |
|---|---|
| $n - 2$ or lower | 1.00 |
| $n - 1$ | 0.85 |
| $n$ (same shell) | 0.35 |

## Radius definitions

- **Van der Waals radius** *(know this)*: when 2 atoms just begin to interact, and you calculate the radius of both.
- **Bonding radius**: when atoms are bonded and their electron clouds overlap.

## Ionization energy

**Ionization energy**: the minimum energy required to remove an e⁻ from the ground state of a gaseous atom or ion.

$$
\ce{X -> X+ + 1e-}
$$

The 2nd I.E. is greater than the 1st I.E. in terms of energy, due to e⁻–e⁻ repulsion; the protons stay the same.

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** the 2nd IE is larger because there is **less** e⁻–e⁻ repulsion after one electron
leaves. The same number of protons now pulls on fewer electrons, so each remaining electron is held
more tightly.
</Callout>

- I.E. is higher for an $n = 1$ electron than for an $n = 3$ electron.
- Larger atomic radius → lower I.E. *(Ryan's note: "less e⁻–e⁻ repulsion")*

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** the trend is right, but the reason is **distance**. In a larger atom the outer
electron is farther from the nucleus, so the attraction is weaker and less energy removes it.
</Callout>

- Core e⁻ feel a higher ENC (Zeff) than valence e⁻: less e⁻ repulsion → a greater + pull from the nucleus.
- Metals have low I.E., so they form cations easily.

## Electron affinity

**Electron affinity**: the energy change that occurs when an e⁻ is added to a gaseous atom or ion.

$$
\ce{X + 1e- -> X-}
$$

- Elements on the left have lower EA, because they want to stabilize their + charge, so less energy is needed to do this.
- Elements on the right side of the P.T. have higher EA, because they are anions and don't want to add another e⁻, so more energy is required to do it.

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** the trend (EA grows toward the right) is right, but the explanation is backward.
Nonmetals on the right, like the halogens, have a high Zeff and **attract** an added electron
strongly, so a lot of energy is **released** when they gain one (a large negative EA). Metals on the
left have a low Zeff, so adding an electron releases little energy. Noble gases are the exception:
an added electron would have to start a new shell, so their EA is positive.
</Callout>

## Electronegativity

**Electronegativity**: an atom's ability to attract e⁻. The most electronegative element is **F**.

## Trend arrows

| Property | Increases going… |
|---|---|
| Ionization energy | right and up |
| Electron affinity | right and up |
| Electronegativity | right and up |
| Atomic size / radius and ionic radius | left and down |

- Higher ENC (the attraction between e⁻ and the nucleus) → smaller ionic radius.
