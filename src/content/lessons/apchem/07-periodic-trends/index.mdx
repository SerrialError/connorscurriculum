## The one-picture summary

| Property | Increases going… | Largest |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | **down** a group and **left** across a period | bottom left (Cs, Fr) |
| Ionization energy | **up** a group and **right** across a period | top right (He) |
| Electron affinity (more negative) | **right** across a period (roughly) | halogens (Cl) |
| Electronegativity | **up** and **right** | F |
| Metallic character | **down** and **left** | bottom left |

Every row is explained by the same two ideas from the last lesson:

1. **Across a period**: same shell, more protons, so a **higher Zeff** pulls the electrons in tighter.
2. **Down a group**: more shells, so the outer electrons are **farther away** and held less tightly.

<Callout type="warning">
Noble gases are usually left out of the electron-affinity and electronegativity trends. He has the
highest **ionization energy**, and F is the most **electronegative**. Those are different properties,
so both statements are true.
</Callout>

## 1. Atomic radius

Atoms don't have hard edges, so radius is defined from distances between nuclei:

- **Bonding atomic radius**: half the distance between the nuclei of two atoms **bonded** together.
  The electron clouds overlap, so this radius is smaller.
- **Nonbonding (van der Waals) radius**: half the distance between two nuclei when the atoms just
  touch without bonding. The clouds don't overlap, so this radius is larger.

A **bond length** can be estimated by adding the two atoms' bonding radii.

<Reveal label="Worked example: estimate a bond length">

The C–C bond is 1.54 Å and the Br–Br bond is 2.28 Å. Estimate the C–Br bond length.

Bonding radius of C $= 1.54 / 2 = 0.77$ Å. Bonding radius of Br $= 2.28 / 2 = 1.14$ Å.

C–Br $\approx 0.77 + 1.14 = 1.91$ Å.

</Reveal>

**Trends.** Radius increases **down a group** (each period adds a shell) and **decreases left to right**
(Zeff rises and pulls the same shell in closer).

## 2. Ionic radius

- **Cations are smaller than their atoms.** Losing electrons often empties the outer shell entirely
  ($\ce{Na}$ is $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^1$, $\ce{Na+}$ is $[\ce{Ne}]$), and with fewer electrons there is less
  electron–electron repulsion, so the rest are pulled in.
- **Anions are larger than their atoms.** The added electron increases electron–electron repulsion,
  and the electrons spread out. The nuclear charge hasn't changed.
- For **isoelectronic** ions (same number of electrons), the one with **more protons is smaller**:

$$
\ce{O^2-} > \ce{F-} > \ce{Na+} > \ce{Mg^2+} > \ce{Al^3+}
$$

All five have 10 electrons, but the protons go from 8 to 13, so the pull on the same electrons gets stronger.

## 3. Ionization energy

**Ionization energy (IE)** is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or
ion in its ground state. Energy always has to go in, so IE is always **positive**.

$$
\text{1st IE:}\ \ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-} \qquad \text{2nd IE:}\ \ce{X+(g) -> X^2+(g) + e-}
$$

**Trends.** IE increases **left to right** (higher Zeff holds the electron more tightly) and **up a
group** (the electron is closer to the nucleus). A bigger atom has a lower IE. **Metals** have low
IEs, which is why they form cations easily.

### Successive ionization energies

$\text{IE}_1 < \text{IE}_2 < \text{IE}_3 < \ldots$ always. Each electron removed leaves the same
number of protons pulling on fewer electrons, so the next one is held more tightly.

<Callout type="warning">
One set of class notes says the 2nd IE is larger "due to e⁻–e⁻ repulsion." It's the reverse: after
one electron is gone there's **less** repulsion, and the positive ion holds its remaining electrons
**more** tightly. That's why the 2nd IE is larger.
</Callout>

A **huge jump** appears when you've removed all the valence electrons and the next one has to come
from the core, a shell closer to the nucleus. **The number of electrons removed before the big jump
equals the number of valence electrons.**

<Reveal label="Worked example: identify the group">

An element in period 3 has these ionization energies (kJ/mol):

| IE₁ | IE₂ | IE₃ | IE₄ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 578 | 1817 | 2745 | 11,577 |

The jump comes between IE₃ and IE₄ (about 4×), so the element has **3 valence electrons**. It's in
group 13: aluminum, which forms $\ce{Al^3+}$.

</Reveal>

### The two dips across a period

The trend has two small exceptions. They're common test questions.

| Dip | Why |
|---|---|
| **Group 2 → 13** (Be > B, Mg > Al) | The electron removed from B or Al is a **p** electron. It's higher in energy than an s electron and partly shielded by the filled s subshell, so it's easier to remove. |
| **Group 15 → 16** (N > O, P > S) | O and S have one **paired** p electron. The repulsion from its partner in the same orbital makes it easier to remove. N and P have three unpaired p electrons, one per orbital. |

<Callout type="ap-tip">
Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES, AP topic 1.6) measures the energy needed to remove electrons from
**every** subshell, not just the outermost one. Each peak is a subshell, its height is how many
electrons are in it, and peaks at higher binding energy come from electrons closer to the nucleus.
The study materials for this class test don't cover PES, but the AP exam does.
</Callout>

## 4. Electron affinity

**Electron affinity (EA)** is the energy change when a gaseous atom **gains** an electron:

$$
\ce{X(g) + e- -> X-(g)}
$$

For most atoms energy is **released**, so EA is **negative**. A more negative EA means the atom
holds the extra electron more strongly.

- EA generally gets **more negative left to right**, because Zeff rises. The **halogens** (group 17)
  have the most negative EAs: one more electron fills their p subshell.
- **Noble gases** have positive EAs (they don't want one): the added electron would have to start a
  new shell, farther out.
- Group 2 (filled s) and group 15 (half-filled p) are less negative than their neighbors, the same
  pattern as the IE dips.
- EA changes little down a group.

<Callout type="warning">
"Higher" or "greater" electron affinity usually means **more negative** (more energy released).
Read the sign carefully. Ionization energy removes an electron (positive); electron affinity adds
one (usually negative).
</Callout>

## 5. Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is an atom's ability to attract the shared electrons in a **bond**. It
increases **up** a group and **right** across a period, for the same reasons as ionization energy.
**Fluorine** is the most electronegative element, followed by O, N, and Cl.

<Callout type="note">
The textbook covers electronegativity in Section 8.4, but the classmate notes include it on this test.
</Callout>

## 6. Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids

- **Metals** (left and bottom): shiny, conduct heat and electricity, low IE, so they **lose**
  electrons and form **cations**.
- **Nonmetals** (upper right): dull, poor conductors, more negative EA, so they tend to **gain**
  electrons and form **anions**.
- **Metalloids** (along the staircase, like Si and Ge) have properties in between.
- Metallic character increases **down and to the left**, the opposite direction from IE.

## 7. Valence electrons and ionic formulas

AP topic 1.8 connects this to Chapter 2: main-group elements lose or gain electrons to reach a
noble-gas configuration, so the group number predicts the ion charge.

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Usual ion | +1 | +2 | +3 | −3 | −2 | −1 |

A group 2 metal and a group 17 nonmetal therefore form $\ce{MX2}$ (like $\ce{CaCl2}$), and elements in
the same group form compounds with the same formula pattern ($\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{KCl}$, $\ce{RbCl}$).
