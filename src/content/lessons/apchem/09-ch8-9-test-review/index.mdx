### Theory checklist

Be able to explain each of these in a sentence or two:

- Why ionic solids have high melting points, are brittle, and conduct only when melted or dissolved.
- How electronegativity differs from electron affinity, and how its trend follows Zeff and distance.
- How ΔEN sorts a bond as nonpolar, polar covalent, or ionic, which atom is δ−, and why that's a continuum, not three boxes.
- What makes one bond dipole larger than another (bigger ΔEN, longer bond), compared without numbers.
- Why the best Lewis structure has formal charges closest to zero, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom.
- How formal charge and oxidation number differ (equal sharing vs. all electrons to the more electronegative atom).
- What a resonance hybrid is, why it doesn't flip between structures, and why its bonds are all the same length.
- Why period 2 atoms never go past an octet while period 3 and lower can, and why $\ce{BF3}$ keeps only 6 electrons on B.
- Why breaking a bond always costs energy, and why a reaction that forms stronger bonds than it breaks is exothermic.
- Why a higher bond order between the same two atoms gives a shorter, stronger bond, and what the minimum of the potential-energy curve tells you.
- Why electron domains spread out, why lone pairs squeeze bond angles, and why lone pairs go in equatorial positions of a trigonal bipyramid.
- Why a molecule with polar bonds can still be nonpolar.
- How σ and π bonds differ, why π is weaker, and why a double bond can't rotate.
- How delocalized π bonds are the orbital picture of resonance.

<Callout type="ap-tip" title="AP exam only: lattice energy">
Lattice energy isn't on the class test, and the practice exam leaves it out. The AP exam asks you to
compare how strongly ions attract using Coulomb's law, which is on the equation sheet:

- **Lattice energy** is the energy to separate 1 mol of an ionic solid into its gaseous ions. It's always positive.
- **Larger ion charges** and **smaller ions** give a larger lattice energy, and charge usually matters more: $\ce{MgO} > \ce{CaO} > \ce{LiF} > \ce{NaCl} > \ce{KCl}$.
- In an answer, name both factors: the **charges** of the ions and the **distance** between them.

The [lattice energy section](../08-ionic-bonding/#5-lattice-energy) of the ionic bonding lesson has the details.
</Callout>

## The equation sheet

The test comes with a periodic table and the official
[AP Chemistry equation sheet](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-chemistry-equations-sheet.pdf).
You don't need to memorize anything that is on it.

**Given on the sheet:**

- Coulomb's law, $F \propto q_1 q_2 / r^2$ (for lattice energy, which is AP only)
- $\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \Delta H^\circ_f\,(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H^\circ_f\,(\text{reactants})$, for formation values (not bond enthalpies)

**Not on the sheet, so memorize:**

- The formal charge formula and the bond-enthalpy estimate below
- Every electron-domain and molecular geometry with its bond angles (the chart below)
- Hybridization from the number of electron domains, and the σ and π count of single, double, and triple bonds
- The electronegativity trend (the periodic table doesn't list values): up and to the right, F highest, then O, then N and Cl
- $1\ \text{Å} = 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$; bond lengths are about 1–2 Å

## Equations

| Equation | Use it for |
|---|---|
| $\text{FC} = V - L - \tfrac{1}{2}B$ | formal charge |
| $\Delta H \approx \sum D_\text{broken} - \sum D_\text{formed}$ | ΔH from bond enthalpies |
| average over the structures | bond order with resonance |

- **Formal charge:** $V$ is the atom's valence electrons, $L$ its lone-pair electrons, and $B$ its
  bonding electrons. Quicker: valence electrons minus dots minus bond lines. The formal charges add up
  to the overall charge.
- **Bond enthalpies:** **broken minus formed**, the reverse of the order for formation values.
- **Bond order with resonance:** carbonate's C–O bonds are $(2 + 1 + 1)/3 = 4/3$, like nitrate's.
  Nitrite and ozone are $(2 + 1)/2 = 3/2$.

## Shapes

The shape chart to know cold. Count electron domains on the central atom (a lone pair or any bond,
single, double, or triple, is one domain), then name the shape from how many are bonded atoms.

<ShapeGrid />

**Bond angles shrink with lone pairs:** $\ce{CH4}$ 109.5°, $\ce{NH3}$ 107°, $\ce{H2O}$ 104.5°. A double
bond also takes more room than a single bond. With 5 domains, lone pairs go **equatorial**. With 6, a
second lone pair goes **opposite** the first.

## Rules and definitions

- **Lewis symbols:** one dot per valence electron. Unpaired dots give the usual number of bonds: C 4, N 3, O 2, F and H 1.
- **Octet rule:** atoms gain, lose, or share electrons to reach 8 valence electrons. H stops at 2.
- **Ions:** ions of groups 1–2, Al, and the nonmetals reach a noble-gas configuration. Transition metals lose their **ns** electrons before (n − 1)d: $\ce{Fe^3+}$ is $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^5$.
- **Bond polarity:** ΔEN of 0 is nonpolar, in between is polar covalent, about 2 or more is ionic. The more electronegative atom is δ−.
- **Drawing a Lewis structure:** count valence electrons (add one per negative charge, subtract one per positive); least electronegative atom in the center (never H or F); in oxyacids, H goes on O; complete outer octets; put leftovers on the center; make multiple bonds if the center is short.
- **Best structure:** formal charges closest to zero, negative charge on the more electronegative atom. Formal charge only compares structures with the same atoms connected.
- **Resonance:** only electrons move, never atoms. Draw the structures with ↔. The real molecule is the hybrid.
- **Octet exceptions:** odd electron count ($\ce{NO}$, $\ce{NO2}$, $\ce{ClO2}$); fewer than 8 on B and Be; more than 8 only for period 3 and lower ($\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{XeF4}$).
- **Bond enthalpy:** the energy to break 1 mol of a bond in the gas phase. Always positive. Table values are averages, so ΔH from them is an estimate.
- **Bond order, length, and strength:** between the same two atoms, triple is shortest and strongest, single is longest and weakest. A double bond is stronger than a single, but not twice as strong.
- **Potential-energy curve:** the minimum's distance is the bond length, and its depth is the bond energy. Higher bond order: deeper and shorter. Larger atoms: longer.
- **Polar molecule:** has polar bonds **and** a shape where the bond dipoles don't cancel. Identical outer atoms with no lone pairs on the center (or lone pairs spread evenly around it, as in $\ce{XeF2}$ and $\ce{XeF4}$) make it nonpolar.
- **σ bond:** head-on overlap, on the line between the nuclei; every bond has exactly one. **π bond:** side-by-side overlap of parallel p orbitals, above and below that line; weaker, and it blocks rotation.

## Common mistakes

1. Forgetting to add electrons for an anion's charge, or to subtract them for a cation.
2. Putting H or F in the center, bonding two O atoms together outside a peroxide, or putting an oxyacid's H on the central atom.
3. Giving C, N, O, or F more than 8 electrons.
4. Counting a double or triple bond as more than one electron domain.
5. Giving the electron-domain geometry when the question asks for the molecular geometry, or the other way around.
6. Putting lone pairs in axial positions of a trigonal bipyramid.
7. Saying a molecule is polar because its bonds are polar, without checking whether the shape cancels them.
8. Saying resonance structures switch back and forth.
9. Doing products minus reactants with bond enthalpies, or forgetting the coefficients when counting bonds.
10. Removing 3d electrons before 4s when making a transition-metal ion.
11. Forgetting the C–H bonds when counting σ bonds.
12. FRQs: give the reasoning, not only the name. Name the number of domains and lone pairs for a shape or angle, and whether bond dipoles cancel for polarity.
