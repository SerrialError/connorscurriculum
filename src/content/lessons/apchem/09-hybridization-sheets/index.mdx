## Sheet 1: Hybridization

### 1. What is hybridization?

Hybridization is the process of mixing atomic orbitals of similar energy to form new hybrid orbitals of
equal energy, which are oriented in space to give the maximum stability to the molecule.

**Key points:**

- It explains the shape of molecules.
- It involves mixing of s, p (and sometimes d) orbitals.
- It forms equal-energy hybrid orbitals.
- It helps in understanding bond angles and geometry.

### 2. Types of hybridization

| Hybridization | Orbitals mixed | Geometry | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| sp | 1s + 1p | linear | 180° |
| sp² | 1s + 2p | trigonal planar | 120° |
| sp³ | 1s + 3p | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| sp³d | 1s + 3p + 1d | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° |
| sp³d² | 1s + 3p + 2d | octahedral | 90° |

<Callout type="note">
"1s + 2p" here means **one s orbital and two p orbitals**, not the 1s and 2p subshells. The geometry
column is the electron-domain geometry; lone pairs can change the molecular geometry (NH₃ is sp³ but
trigonal pyramidal).
</Callout>

### 3. Examples

**a) sp hybridization (BeCl₂).** Be: 1s² 2s² → 2s + 2p. Linear shape (180°): Cl–Be–Cl.

**b) sp² hybridization (BF₃).** B: 1s² 2s² 2p¹ → 2s + 2pₓ + 2p_y. Trigonal planar (120°).

**c) sp³ hybridization (CH₄).** C: 1s² 2s² 2p² → 2s + 2pₓ + 2p_y + 2p_z. Tetrahedral shape (109.5°).

**d) sp³d² hybridization (SF₆).** S: 3s² 3p⁴ 3d² → sp³d². Octahedral shape (90°).

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** ground-state sulfur is 3s² 3p⁴ with no electrons in 3d. To make six bonds, one 3s and one
3p electron are promoted, giving **3s¹ 3p³ 3d²** with six unpaired electrons. Those six orbitals mix into
six sp³d² orbitals.
</Callout>

### 4. Important points

- Hybridization does not change the number of atoms.
- It only explains the arrangement and bonding.
- The greater the s-character, the higher the stability of the hybrid orbital.
- Order of s-character: sp (50%) > sp² (33.3%) > sp³ (25%).

**Hybridization = Structure + Shape + Stability**

## Sheet 2: King's Formula (by atulsirmaths)

Hybridization is defined as the mixing of the atomic orbitals belonging to the same atom but having
slightly different energies, so that a redistribution of energy takes place between them, resulting in
the formation of new orbitals of equal energies and identical shapes. The new orbitals thus formed are
known as hybrid orbitals and are more stable.

(See the correction under Sheet 3: hybrid orbitals aren't lower in energy than the orbitals they come from.)

### Method for finding the hybridization

Apply this formula to find the hybridization of the central atom:

$$Z = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\text{valence } e^- \text{ of central atom} + \text{monovalent atoms attached} - \text{negative charge} - \text{positive charge}\right)$$

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** a **negative** charge is **added**, not subtracted. The sheet's own sulfate example adds
it. The formula is

$$Z = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(V + M + \text{anion charge} - \text{cation charge}\right)$$

where $M$ counts only atoms that form one bond (H and the halogens). Oxygen and sulfur count as 0.
</Callout>

| Value of Z | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hybridization | sp | sp² | sp³ | sp³d | sp³d² | sp³d³ |

**Examples:**

- Hybridization of N in NH₃ $= \tfrac{1}{2}[5 + 3 + 0 - 0] = 4$ ⇒ sp³
- Hybridization of S in SO₄²⁻ $= \tfrac{1}{2}[6 + 0 + 2 - 0] = 4$ ⇒ sp³

<Callout type="note">
When it works, Z is the number of electron domains on the central atom (try XeF₄: ½(8 + 4) = 6,
sp³d²). It only works when every outer atom is H, a halogen, O, or S. It fails for HCN and C₂H₂ (the
sheet's own sp example): both give ½(4 + 1) = 2.5. It also fails for N in HNO₃ and for odd-electron
molecules like NO₂. Counting domains on the Lewis structure always works, and you need the Lewis
structure anyway to name the molecular geometry. Z = 7 (sp³d³, IF₇) is beyond what your class covers.
</Callout>

### Some common types of hybridization, with shapes and examples

| Type | Atomic orbitals involved | Shape and bond angles | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| sp | one s + one p | linear, 180° | BeCl₂, BeH₂, C₂H₂ |
| sp² | one s + two p | triangular (trigonal) planar, 120° | BF₃, BCl₃, C₂H₄, CO₃²⁻ |
| sp³ | one s + three p | tetrahedral, 109°28′ | CH₄, CCl₄, SnCl₄, NH₄⁺ |
| dsp² | one d + one s + two p | square planar, 90° | XeF₄ |
| sp³d | one s + three p + one d | trigonal bipyramidal, 90° and 120° | PCl₅, PF₅ |
| sp³d² | one s + three p + two d | octahedral, 90° | SF₆, [CrF₆]³⁻ |

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** XeF₄ is **sp³d²**, not dsp². Xe has four bonds and two lone pairs, six domains, and the
sheet's own formula gives Z = 6. Its shape is square planar because the two lone pairs sit opposite each
other in an octahedron. dsp² describes some transition-metal complexes, which this course doesn't cover. The
sheet's [CrF₆]³⁻ example is also wrong: in valence-bond terms it's d²sp³, not sp³d². Use SF₆ as the
sp³d² example.
</Callout>

109°28′ is 109 degrees 28 minutes, the same as 109.5°.

## Sheet 3: Hybridization

### What is hybridization?

- Hybridization is the process of mixing atomic orbitals of similar energy and comparable size of an
  atom to form an equal number of hybrid orbitals of the same energy, shape, and orientation.
- These hybrid orbitals are more stable than the original atomic orbitals and are used for bond formation.

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** a hybrid orbital isn't lower in energy than the orbitals it was made from. Its energy is
between the s and p energies (sp³ is closer to p, sp closer to s). What hybridization gains is shape:
hybrid orbitals point straight at the other atoms, so they overlap better and form stronger bonds. Sheet
1's "more s-character, more stable" is about comparing hybrids with each other, which is fine.
</Callout>

**Key points:**

1. Hybrid orbitals are formed by the combination of atomic orbitals of the same atom.
2. Number of hybrid orbitals = number of atomic orbitals mixed.
3. The hybrid orbitals have equal energy and are degenerate.
4. Hybridization explains the geometry, directionality, and bond angles of molecules.

### Types of hybridization

| Type | Atomic orbitals mixed | No. of hybrid orbitals | Geometry | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sp | 1s + 1p | 2 | linear | 180° |
| sp² | 1s + 2p | 3 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| sp³ | 1s + 3p | 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| sp³d | 1s + 3p + 1d | 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° |
| sp³d² | 1s + 3p + 2d | 6 | octahedral | 90° |

**1. sp hybridization.** One s and one p orbital mix to form 2 sp hybrid orbitals. Geometry: linear.
Bond angle: 180°. Examples: BeCl₂, CO₂, C₂H₂.

**2. sp² hybridization.** One s and two p orbitals mix to form 3 sp² hybrid orbitals. Geometry: trigonal
planar. Bond angle: 120°. Examples: BF₃, SO₃, C₂H₄.

**3. sp³ hybridization.** One s and three p orbitals mix to form 4 sp³ hybrid orbitals. Geometry:
tetrahedral. Bond angle: 109.5°. Examples: CH₄, CCl₄, NH₃, H₂O.

**4. sp³d hybridization.** One s, three p, and one d (d_z²) orbital mix to form 5 sp³d hybrid orbitals.
Geometry: trigonal bipyramidal. Bond angles: 90°, 120°. Examples: PCl₅, SF₄.

**5. sp³d² hybridization.** One s, three p, and two d orbitals mix to form 6 sp³d² hybrid orbitals.
Geometry: octahedral. Bond angle: 90°. Examples: SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻.

**Remember:** sp → linear; sp² → trigonal planar; sp³ → tetrahedral; sp³d → trigonal bipyramidal;
sp³d² → octahedral.

<Callout type="warning">
**Corrections:**

- The sheet's orbital box diagrams don't keep the electron count. Its sp² diagram starts with four
  electrons (2s ↑↓, 2p ↑ ↑) and ends with three (sp² ↑ ↑ ↑). In BF₃, boron has **three** valence
  electrons: 2s² 2p¹ → three sp² orbitals with one electron each. Carbon in C₂H₄ keeps its fourth electron
  in the p orbital that isn't hybridized, and that electron forms the π bond.
- The sheet's sp diagram (2s ↑↓, 2p ↑) has three electrons, but its example, Be in BeCl₂, has two:
  2s² → two sp orbitals with one electron each.
- [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ is d²sp³, not sp³d², and transition-metal complexes aren't part of this course. Use SF₆ as
  the sp³d² example.
</Callout>
