These are the 20 formulas from the class lab handout, *Lab: Lewis Dot Structures (Scholars)*. Try each one
on paper (or with the model kit) first, then open the answer to check it. Every answer has a 3D model you
can turn, so you can compare it with the one you built.

<Callout type="note">
The lab's blank "# valence e⁻ pairs on central atom" means **electron domains**: lone pairs plus bonded
atoms, with a double or triple bond counting as one. That's how the lab's own table uses it: CO₂ has 2,
even though C shares four pairs.
</Callout>

## How to fill in each blank

1. **Lewis structure.** Count valence electrons (add one for each − charge, take one away for each +),
   put the least electronegative atom in the center (never H), connect with single bonds, fill the outer
   atoms' octets, put leftovers on the center, and make multiple bonds if the center is short.
2. **Valence e⁻ pairs on the central atom:** bonded atoms + lone pairs on the central atom.
3. **Electronic geometry** from that number: 2 linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal
   bipyramidal, 6 octahedral.
4. **Molecular geometry:** the same, named by where the atoms are (see the
   [VSEPR lesson](../09-vsepr/) for the full chart).
5. **Bond angles:** the ideal angle for the electronic geometry, a little less if the central atom has
   lone pairs (except square planar and linear, which stay at 90° and 180°).
6. **Polar or nonpolar:** nonpolar only if the bond dipoles cancel: each bond dipole needs an equal
   partner pointing the opposite way. Lone pairs on the central atom usually break that symmetry.

The lab says items 9, 10, 16, 17, and 18 break the octet rule: their central atoms have 10 or 12
electrons.

### 2. HCN

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $1 + 4 + 5 = 10$ electrons. H–C≡N with one lone pair on N. C is central: H can only make one bond.

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 2 |
| Electronic geometry | linear |
| Molecular geometry | linear |
| Bond angles (approx.) | 180° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **polar** |

The two ends are different (H and N), so the bond dipoles add instead of cancelling.

### 3. H₂S

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

### 4. CO₂

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $4 + 2(6) = 16$ electrons. O=C=O with two lone pairs on each O.

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 2 |
| Electronic geometry | linear |
| Molecular geometry | linear |
| Bond angles (approx.) | 180° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **nonpolar** |

The two C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel. Each double bond is one domain, so C has 2 domains even though it has 4 bonding pairs.

</Reveal>

### 5. SeO₃²⁻

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $6 + 3(6) + 2 = 26$ electrons. Se with three Se–O single bonds, three lone pairs on each O, and one lone pair on Se. Brackets and a 2− charge around the whole thing.

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 4 |
| Electronic geometry | tetrahedral |
| Molecular geometry | trigonal pyramidal |
| Bond angles (approx.) | a little under 109.5° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **polar** |

Pyramidal, like NH₃. Formal charges are +1 on Se and −1 on each O; some books add Se=O double bonds to lower them, which doesn't change the shape.

### 6. HNO₃

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $1 + 5 + 3(6) = 24$ electrons. N in the center (as the lab says), bonded to three O; the H is on one of the O atoms. N has one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds. Lone pairs: 2 on the double-bonded O, 3 on the O without the H, 2 on the O–H oxygen. Formal charges: +1 on N, −1 on the O with three lone pairs.

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 3 |
| Electronic geometry | trigonal planar |
| Molecular geometry | trigonal planar |
| Bond angles (approx.) | 120° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **polar** |

The three groups on N are different (=O, –O⁻, –OH), so the dipoles don't cancel. There are two resonance structures: the N=O can go to either O that doesn't have the H.

<Molecule id="hno3" />

</Reveal>

### 7. CH₂Cl₂

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|

### 9. ICl₄⁻

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $7 + 4(7) + 1 = 36$ electrons. Four I–Cl single bonds use 8; the Cl lone pairs use 24; the last 4 go on I as two lone pairs, so I has 12 electrons around it (an expanded octet).

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 6 |
| Electronic geometry | octahedral |
| Molecular geometry | square planar |
| Bond angles (approx.) | 90° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **nonpolar** |

The two lone pairs sit opposite each other, leaving a flat square of Cl atoms whose dipoles cancel.

### 10. SCl₆

<Reveal label="Show the answer">

**Lewis structure:** $6 + 6(7) = 48$ electrons. Six S–Cl single bonds and three lone pairs on each Cl; no lone pairs on S, which has 12 electrons around it.

| On the lab sheet | Answer |
|---|---|
| # valence e⁻ pairs on central atom | 6 |
| Electronic geometry | octahedral |
| Molecular geometry | octahedral |
| Bond angles (approx.) | 90° |
| Polar or nonpolar | **nonpolar** |

All six positions are the same atom, so every dipole has an opposite one to cancel. (SF₆ is the real compound with this shape; six Cl atoms are too big to fit around S, but the lab treats it the same way.)

### 12. ClO₃⁻
