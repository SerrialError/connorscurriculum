## 1. Bond dipoles add like arrows

Chapter 8 showed that a bond between atoms of different electronegativity has a **bond dipole**. A
whole molecule's **dipole moment** is the sum of its bond dipoles, and because each one has a
**direction**, they add like arrows (vectors), not like plain numbers. Two equal arrows pointing opposite
ways add up to zero.

So whether a molecule is polar depends on two things:

1. whether it has **polar bonds**, and
2. its **shape**, which decides whether those bond dipoles cancel.

| Molecule | Shape | Bond dipoles | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | linear | the two C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel | **nonpolar** |
| $\ce{H2O}$ | bent | both O–H dipoles point partly toward O, so they add | **polar** |
| $\ce{BF3}$ | trigonal planar | three equal dipoles 120° apart cancel | **nonpolar** |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | trigonal pyramidal | the three N–H dipoles all tilt toward N | **polar** |
| $\ce{CCl4}$ | tetrahedral | four equal dipoles cancel | **nonpolar** |
| $\ce{CHCl3}$ | tetrahedral | C–H and C–Cl dipoles are different, so they don't cancel | **polar** |

CO₂ and H₂O are the classic pair: both have two polar bonds, but only the bent one is polar.

![Bond dipoles drawn as arrows. In linear CO₂ the two arrows point away from C toward each O, in opposite directions, so they cancel and the molecule is nonpolar. In bent H₂O both arrows point from H toward O, and their sum is a net dipole pointing toward O, so the molecule is polar.](./dipoles-co2-h2o.svg)

## 2. A quick check

A molecule is **nonpolar** if either is true:

- all its bonds are nonpolar (like $\ce{Cl2}$ or the C–C and C–H framework of hydrocarbons, which are treated
  as nonpolar), or
- the central atom is surrounded **symmetrically**: all the outer atoms are the **same**, and there are
  **no lone pairs** on the central atom, or the lone pairs are arranged symmetrically (opposite each other, or evenly spaced around the middle).

The second case covers the basic shapes (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal,
octahedral) with identical outer atoms, plus two shapes that do have lone pairs:

- $\ce{XeF2}$, **linear**: three equatorial lone pairs, and the two F atoms are opposite each other.
- $\ce{XeF4}$, **square planar**: the two lone pairs are opposite each other, and so are each pair of F atoms.

Everything else with polar bonds is **usually polar**: bent, trigonal pyramidal, seesaw, T-shaped,
square pyramidal, and most shapes where the outer atoms aren't all the same. When the outer atoms
differ, check whether each bond dipole has an equal partner pointing the opposite way.

$\ce{CH2Cl2}$ is tetrahedral, but the two Cl atoms are on one side of the carbon and the two H atoms on
the other, so the bond dipoles can't cancel:

<Reveal label="Worked example: polar or nonpolar?">

| Molecule | Shape | Polar? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{SO2}$ | bent (3 domains, 1 lone pair) | polar | the lone pair breaks the symmetry |
| $\ce{SO3}$ | trigonal planar | nonpolar | three identical S–O directions 120° apart |
| $\ce{CH2Cl2}$ | tetrahedral | polar | two kinds of outer atoms |
| $\ce{SF4}$ | seesaw | polar | the equatorial lone pair breaks the symmetry |
| $\ce{PCl5}$ | trigonal bipyramidal | nonpolar | identical atoms in a symmetric arrangement |
| $\ce{BrF5}$ | square pyramidal | polar | one lone pair with nothing opposite it |

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
"It has polar bonds" is not the same as "it's a polar molecule." On a test, say both parts: the bonds
are polar **because of the electronegativity difference**, and the molecule is polar or nonpolar
**because the shape makes the bond dipoles add or cancel**.
</Callout>

## 3. Why it matters

Polarity decides how molecules attract **each other**. Polar molecules line up their δ+ and δ− ends,
which affects boiling points and what dissolves in what. That's the subject of Chapter 11
(intermolecular forces).

<Callout type="ap-tip">
On the AP exam, a polarity answer that earns credit names the **shape** (or says whether there are lone
pairs on the central atom) and explains whether the **bond dipoles cancel**. Drawing a Lewis structure
first is the safest route.
</Callout>
