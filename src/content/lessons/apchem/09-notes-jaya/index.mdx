## 9.1 Molecular Shapes

- Lewis structures do not indicate shape, so we use molecular geometry (MG).
- The shape of a molecule is determined by its bond angles.
- Use a chart to determine. (Jaya's chart merges cells that repeat; here every row is filled in.)

| 2 | 2 | 0 | linear | linear | 180° | sp |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | trigonal planar | 120° | sp² |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | trigonal planar | bent or angular | 120° | sp² |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | tetrahedral | 109.5° | sp³ |

<Callout type="note">
The bond angles in the chart are the ideal ones. Lone pairs push the bonds a little closer together, so
a molecule with lone pairs on the central atom has angles slightly **smaller** than these (NH₃ is about
107°, H₂O about 104.5°). The exceptions are square planar and the linear shape from 5 domains, where
the lone pairs sit opposite each other and the angles stay exactly 90° and 180°.

Five domains also has a fourth shape that isn't in the chart: 2 bonding pairs and 3 nonbonding pairs is
**linear** (XeF₂, I₃⁻).
</Callout>

## 9.2 VSEPR Model

- **Bonding pair:** where electrons are most likely located.
  - Determines the electron domain, defined as in H–N–H with an H below the N: the three N–H bonds are
    bonding pairs, and the pair on N is a nonbonding pair.
- **Nonbonding pair:** a lone pair (on one atom).
- Each nonbonding pair, single bond, or multiple bond produces a single electron domain around the central atom.
- VSEPR model idea: electron domains are both (−), so they repel each other.
- **Electron-domain geometry:** the 3D arrangement of the electron domains around an atom according to VSEPR.
- **Molecular geometry:** the arrangement in space of the atoms of a molecule.

**To determine structure:**

1. Draw the Lewis structure, counting electron domains around the central atom. Each bond counts as only
   one electron domain.
2. Use the table and the number of electron domains.
3. Use the arrangement of bonded atoms to find the molecular geometry.

## 9.3 Molecular Shape & Polarity

- The dipole moment depends on both the polarities of the individual bonds and the geometry of the molecule.
- The dipole moment of a polyatomic molecule is the vector sum of its bond dipoles (nonpolar: they cancel
  out), e.g. $\ce{CO2}$ ⇒ 0 dipole.

### Relationship between shape and polarity

| ABₙ | Nonpolar | Polar |
|---|---|---|
| AB₂ | linear | bent |
| AB₃ | trigonal planar | trigonal pyramidal, T-shaped |
| AB₄ | tetrahedral, square planar | see-saw |
| AB₅ | trigonal bipyramidal | |
| AB₆ | octahedral | square pyramidal |

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** a square pyramid has **five** atoms around the center (BrF₅), so square pyramidal belongs
in the **AB₅** row, not AB₆. AB₆ only has the octahedral shape.

This table also assumes all the B atoms are the same. When they aren't, a "nonpolar" shape can be polar:
$\ce{CH2Cl2}$ is tetrahedral but polar, because the C–H and C–Cl bond dipoles don't cancel.
</Callout>

## 9.4 Covalent Bonding and Orbital Overlap

- **Valence-bond theory:** a model of chemical bonding in which an electron-pair bond is formed between
  two atoms by the overlap of orbitals on the two atoms.
- **Overlap:** the extent to which atomic orbitals on different atoms share the same region of space. Big
  overlap ⇒ strong bond.
- Overlap allows 2 electrons of opposite spin to share the space between the nuclei, forming a covalent bond.
- For example: s + s (as in $\ce{H2}$) or s + p (as in $\ce{HCl}$), etc.
- Potential energy decreases with increasing orbital overlap.
- Nucleus–nucleus repulsion: Jaya sketched two atoms side by side (higher potential energy) and two
  atoms with overlapping orbitals (lower potential energy).
- Stronger bonds mean less potential energy because of electrostatic repulsion.

<Callout type="warning">
**Correction:** the energy is lowest at the bond length because the **attraction** of both nuclei for the
shared electrons is greatest there compared with the repulsion. Overlap lowers the energy only down to that
point: closer than the bond length, nucleus–nucleus **repulsion** takes over and the energy rises. A
stronger bond has a deeper energy minimum.
</Callout>

## 9.5 Hybrid Orbitals

- **Hybrid orbitals:** orbitals that result from the mixing of different kinds of atomic orbitals on the
  same atom, e.g. sp³ = s + p + p + p.
- **Hybridization:** the mathematical mixing of different types of atomic orbitals to produce a set of
  equivalent hybrid orbitals.

**For example, BeF₂:** Jaya wrote it as Be²⁺(F⁻)₂ ⇒ :F̤–Be–F̤: with Be = 1s²2s².

<Callout type="note">
BeF₂ is mostly ionic in the solid, but the textbook treats a single gas-phase F–Be–F molecule as covalent
to show sp hybridization. That's the version used here.
</Callout>

| Ground state | ↑↓ | ↑↓ | ☐ ☐ ☐ | no electrons to bond |

- In the ground state there are no unpaired electrons to bond, so electronic excitement is necessary.
