A Lewis structure shows which atoms are bonded, but not the molecule's shape. The **valence-shell
electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) model** predicts the shape from one idea: **groups of electrons around a
central atom repel each other, so they spread out as far apart as possible.**

## 1. Electron domains

An **electron domain** is a region around the central atom where electrons are concentrated. Count:

- each **lone pair** as one domain, and
- each **bond** as one domain, **whether it's single, double, or triple**.

<Reveal label="Worked example: count domains">

| Molecule | Around the central atom | Domains |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | two double bonds | 2 |
| $\ce{SO2}$ | one double bond, one single bond, one lone pair on S | 3 |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | three single bonds, one lone pair | 4 |
| $\ce{SF4}$ | four single bonds, one lone pair | 5 |
| $\ce{XeF4}$ | four single bonds, two lone pairs | 6 |

</Reveal>

The book also labels shapes with **ABₙ** notation: A is the central atom and n is the number of B
atoms bonded to it, so $\ce{CH4}$ is AB₄ and $\ce{NH3}$ is AB₃. Some teachers add Eₘ for lone pairs
(NH₃ is AB₃E).

## 2. Electron-domain geometry

The number of domains sets how they're arranged in space:

| Domains | Electron-domain geometry | Bond angles |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120°, and 180° |
| 6 | octahedral | 90° and 180° |

## 3. Molecular geometry

The **molecular geometry** describes where the **atoms** are, not the lone pairs. The lone pairs still
take up space, but you can't "see" them in the shape. When there are no lone pairs on the central atom,
the two geometries are the same.

| Domains | Bonding | Lone pairs | Molecular geometry | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 0 | linear | $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{HCN}$, $\ce{BeCl2}$ |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | $\ce{BF3}$, $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{NO3-}$ |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | bent | $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{O3}$, $\ce{NO2-}$ |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{PCl3}$, $\ce{H3O+}$ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | bent | $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{OF2}$ |
| 5 | 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | $\ce{PCl5}$ |
| 5 | 4 | 1 | seesaw | $\ce{SF4}$ |
| 5 | 3 | 2 | T-shaped | $\ce{ClF3}$ |
| 5 | 2 | 3 | linear | $\ce{XeF2}$, $\ce{I3-}$ |
| 6 | 6 | 0 | octahedral | $\ce{SF6}$ |
| 6 | 5 | 1 | square pyramidal | $\ce{BrF5}$ |
| 6 | 4 | 2 | square planar | $\ce{XeF4}$, $\ce{ICl4-}$ |

![Sketches of all 13 molecular geometries, grouped by number of electron domains. Lone pairs are drawn as shaded lobes; dashed bonds point away from the viewer. Five domains: lone pairs sit in the middle (equatorial) positions, giving seesaw, T-shaped, and linear. Six domains: one lone pair gives square pyramidal, and two opposite lone pairs give square planar.](./vsepr-shapes.svg)

**The procedure:**

1. Draw the Lewis structure.
2. Count the electron domains on the central atom and name the electron-domain geometry.
3. Name the molecular geometry from how many of those domains are bonds to atoms.

<Reveal label="Worked example: ClF₃">

1. $7 + 3(7) = 28$ electrons. Cl is central with three Cl–F bonds (6), and each F gets three lone pairs
   (18), for 24. The last 4 go on Cl as two lone pairs.
2. Cl has 3 bonds + 2 lone pairs = **5 domains**, so the electron-domain geometry is **trigonal
   bipyramidal**.
3. Three are bonds, so the molecular geometry is **T-shaped**.

</Reveal>

<Callout type="warning">
"Bent" shows up twice: with 3 domains (one lone pair, angle a bit under 120°) and with 4 domains (two
lone pairs, angle a bit under 109.5°). Say which one when you give a bond angle.
</Callout>

## 4. What changes bond angles

The ideal angles above assume every domain is the same size. They aren't:

- **Lone pairs take up more room than bonding pairs.** A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it
  spreads out more and pushes the bonds closer together. Each lone pair added to a tetrahedral domain
  set shrinks the angle:

  | $\ce{CH4}$ | $\ce{NH3}$ | $\ce{H2O}$ |
  |---|---|---|
  | 109.5° | 107° | 104.5° |

- **Multiple bonds take up more room than single bonds.** They hold more electrons. In a molecule like
  $\ce{Cl2C=O}$, the Cl–C–Cl angle is **less than 120°** and the Cl–C=O angles are **more than 120°**.

### Where lone pairs go with 5 and 6 domains

In a **trigonal bipyramid**, the two **axial** positions (top and bottom) each have three neighbors at
90°, while the three **equatorial** positions (around the middle) have only two. Lone pairs need more
room, so they **always go in equatorial positions**. That's why one lone pair gives a seesaw, two give a
T shape, and three give a linear molecule.

In an **octahedron**, all six positions are equivalent, so the first lone pair can go anywhere. A
second lone pair goes **opposite** the first (180° away), which leaves the four atoms in a **square
plane**.

## 5. Larger molecules

In a molecule with more than one "central" atom, apply VSEPR to **each interior atom separately**.

<Reveal label="Worked example: acetic acid, CH₃COOH">

The skeleton is H₃C–C(=O)–O–H.

| Atom | Domains | Geometry around it | Angles |
|---|---|---|---|
| C in CH₃ | 4 bonds | tetrahedral | about 109.5° |
| C in C=O | 3 bonds (one double) | trigonal planar | about 120° |
| O in O–H | 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs | bent | about 105° |

</Reveal>

<Callout type="ap-tip">
AP questions often ask you to justify a bond angle. Name the number of electron domains, say they repel
and get as far apart as possible, and mention any lone pairs, which repel more strongly than bonding
pairs. A geometry name with no reasoning usually doesn't earn the point.
</Callout>
