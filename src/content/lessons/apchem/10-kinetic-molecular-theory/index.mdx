The gas laws describe **what** gases do. **Kinetic-molecular theory** (KMT) explains **why**, using a
simple model of moving particles.

## 1. The assumptions of the model (10.6)

1. A gas is made of a huge number of particles in **continuous, random motion**.
2. The **volume of the particles themselves is negligible** compared with the volume of the container.
3. The particles **don't attract or repel each other**.
4. Collisions are **elastic**: energy can pass from one particle to another, but the total kinetic energy
   doesn't change as long as the temperature stays the same.
5. The **average kinetic energy** of the particles is **proportional to the absolute (kelvin)
   temperature**.

An ideal gas is one that fits these assumptions exactly. Assumptions 2 and 3 are the ones real gases
break, which is the subject of the [Real Gases lesson](../10-real-gases/).

## 2. Temperature is average kinetic energy (10.6)

Assumption 5 is what temperature **means** at the particle level. Double the kelvin temperature and you
double the average kinetic energy of the particles.

$$
KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

(This is on the equation sheet.) Two consequences come up constantly:

- **At the same temperature, every gas has the same average kinetic energy.** He, $\ce{N2}$, and $\ce{CO2}$
  at 300 K all have the same average $KE$ per particle.
- **So lighter particles move faster.** If $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ is the same and $m$ is smaller, $v$ must be
  larger. At the same temperature, helium atoms move about 3.2 times as fast on average as argon atoms,
  because $\sqrt{39.95/4.003} = 3.16$.

<Callout type="warning">
"Same temperature" means same average kinetic energy, **not** same speed. And doubling the kelvin
temperature doubles the kinetic energy but raises the average speed only by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$, about
1.4.
</Callout>

## 3. Maxwell–Boltzmann distributions (10.6, AP topic 3.5)

Not every particle moves at the average speed. Collisions constantly speed some up and slow others down.
A **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** shows how many particles have each speed (or each kinetic energy).

![Speed distributions for N₂ at 200 K, 300 K, and 600 K. Each curve rises from zero, peaks, and tails off to the right. The 200 K curve has the tallest, narrowest peak, at about 345 m/s. The 300 K peak is at about 420 m/s, and the 600 K curve has the lowest, widest peak, at about 600 m/s. All three curves enclose the same area.](./mb-temperature.svg)

How to read it:

- The **peak** is the most probable speed.
- The **area under the curve** is the total number of particles. Same sample, same area.
- **Higher temperature:** the peak moves right (faster), and since the area can't change, the curve gets
  **lower and wider**. More particles have high speeds.

![Speed distributions at 300 K for Xe (131 g/mol), Ar (40 g/mol), Ne (20 g/mol), and He (4 g/mol). Xe has the tallest, narrowest peak at the lowest speed, about 195 m/s. Ar peaks near 350 m/s, Ne near 500 m/s, and He has a low, very wide curve peaking near 1100 m/s.](./mb-molar-mass.svg)

- **Lower molar mass, same temperature:** the curve looks like the high-temperature one. The peak moves
  right and the curve spreads out.

<Callout type="ap-tip">
When asked to sketch the distribution for a higher temperature, draw the peak **to the right and lower**
than the original, with a longer tail to the right, and keep the area about the same. A taller peak at
higher temperature is the most common error.

One subtle point: if the x-axis is **kinetic energy** instead of speed, two different gases at the same
temperature have the **same** curve, because they have the same distribution of kinetic energies. Only the
speed distributions differ.
</Callout>

## 4. How KMT explains the gas laws (10.6)

Each law follows from one idea: **pressure comes from collisions with the walls**, and it depends on how
often the particles hit and how hard.

| Change | What the particles do | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Decrease $V$ ($n$, $T$ constant) | Same speeds, less room: more collisions per second on each part of the wall | $P$ increases (Boyle) |
| Increase $T$ ($n$, $V$ constant) | Faster particles hit more often and harder | $P$ increases |
| Increase $T$ ($n$, $P$ constant) | Collisions become harder and more frequent, so the gas expands until the pressure is back where it was | $V$ increases (Charles) |
| Add gas ($T$, $V$ constant) | More particles, more collisions | $P$ increases |
| Mix gases | Particles don't interact, so each gas hits the walls as if alone | Partial pressures add (Dalton) |

<Reveal label="Worked example: explaining a pressure change">

**Question:** A rigid container of gas is heated from 300 K to 400 K. Explain, in terms of the particles, why
the pressure increases.

**Answer:** At higher temperature the average kinetic energy of the particles is greater, so they move
faster. They collide with the walls more often, and with more force, so the pressure increases. The volume
can't change because the container is rigid.

</Reveal>

## 5. Effusion and diffusion (10.7)

**Effusion** is gas escaping through a tiny hole into a vacuum, one particle at a time. **Diffusion** is one
gas spreading through another. Both are faster for **lighter** particles, because lighter particles move
faster at the same temperature. That's why a helium balloon goes flat faster than an air-filled one.

![Left: effusion. Gas particles in one half of a box pass one at a time through a tiny hole into a vacuum in the other half. Right: diffusion. Ammonia (17.0 g/mol) enters one end of a glass tube and hydrogen chloride (36.5 g/mol) enters the other. Where they meet, a white ring of solid ammonium chloride forms, closer to the HCl end, about 59% of the way from the NH₃ end, because the lighter ammonia travels farther in the same time.](./effusion-diffusion.svg)

Diffusion is much slower than the particles' speeds suggest. A nitrogen molecule at room temperature moves
at about 500 m/s, but a smell takes seconds or minutes to cross a room. The molecule keeps colliding with
other molecules and changing direction. The average distance it travels between collisions is the **mean
free path**, about 70 nm in air at sea level.

<Callout type="not-on-ap" title="Graham's law is class-only">
The AP exam asks you to explain **qualitatively** that lighter gases effuse and diffuse faster (topic 3.5).
The calculations below aren't in the AP course description or on the equation sheet. They are in the
textbook and may be on the class test.
</Callout>

**Graham's law of effusion:** the rates of effusion of two gases at the same temperature and pressure are
inversely proportional to the square roots of their molar masses:

$$
\frac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{M}_2}{\mathcal{M}_1}}
$$

The time to effuse a fixed amount is the reverse: the heavier gas takes longer, by the same factor.

The **root-mean-square speed** is one way to describe a typical particle speed:

$$
u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{\mathcal{M}}}
$$

Use $R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K) and $\mathcal{M}$ in **kg/mol** so that the speed comes out in m/s.

<Reveal label="Worked example: Graham's law">

**(a)** How many times faster does He effuse than $\ce{O2}$ at the same conditions?

$$
\frac{r_\text{He}}{r_{\ce{O2}}} = \sqrt{\frac{32.00}{4.003}} = 2.83
$$

**(b)** An unknown gas takes 4.00 times as long as He to effuse. What is its molar mass?

The unknown's rate is $1/4.00$ of helium's, so

$$
\frac{r_\text{He}}{r_x} = 4.00 = \sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{M}_x}{4.003}} \qquad \mathcal{M}_x = 4.003 \times 4.00^2 = 64.0\ \text{g/mol}
$$

That matches $\ce{SO2}$ (64.07 g/mol).

**(c)** What is $u_\text{rms}$ for $\ce{N2}$ at 25 °C?

$$
u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3(8.314)(298.15)}{0.02802}} = 515\ \text{m/s}
$$

</Reveal>
