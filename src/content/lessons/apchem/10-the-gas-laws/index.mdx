Four quantities describe a sample of gas: pressure $P$, volume $V$, temperature $T$, and amount $n$ (in
moles). Each gas law holds two of them constant and says how the other two are related.

## 1. Temperature has to be in kelvins

Every gas law uses **absolute temperature**:

$$
\text{K} = {}^\circ\text{C} + 273.15
$$

(This is on the equation sheet.) The reason shows up in Charles's law below: a gas's volume is
proportional to its temperature only when temperature is measured from absolute zero. Going from 20 °C to
40 °C doesn't double anything. Going from 293 K to 586 K does.

## 2. Boyle's law: pressure and volume (10.3)

At constant temperature and amount of gas, **pressure and volume are inversely proportional**. Halve the
volume and the pressure doubles.

$$
P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 \qquad (n, T \text{ constant})
$$

**Particle view:** the particles still move at the same speeds (same temperature), but in half the space
they hit each part of the wall twice as often.

![Three piston cylinders holding the same 8 particles. Center, the start. Left, the volume is halved at the same temperature: the particles move at the same speeds but hit the walls twice as often, so the pressure doubles. Right, the kelvin temperature is doubled at the same pressure: the particles move faster and the piston rises until the volume has doubled.](./piston-particles.svg)

<Reveal label="Worked example: Boyle's law">

A gas occupies 2.50 L at 1.20 atm. At constant temperature, it is compressed to 0.800 L. What is the new
pressure?

$$
P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{(1.20\ \text{atm})(2.50\ \text{L})}{0.800\ \text{L}} = 3.75\ \text{atm}
$$

Check the direction: the volume went down, so the pressure should go up. It did.

</Reveal>

## 3. Charles's law: volume and temperature (10.3)

At constant pressure and amount, **volume is proportional to absolute temperature**:

$$
\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} \qquad (n, P \text{ constant})
$$

**Particle view:** hotter particles move faster and hit the walls harder and more often. To keep the
pressure the same, the gas has to spread out until the collisions on each part of the wall are back to
what they were. A balloon taken outside on a cold day shrinks for this reason.

If you plot the volume of a gas against temperature in °C and extend the line to the left, it reaches zero
volume at **−273.15 °C**, whatever the gas or its amount. That temperature is **absolute zero**, 0 K. (Real
gases condense to liquids before they get there, which is why the lines below are dashed at the left.)

<Reveal label="Worked example: Charles's law">

A balloon holds 1.50 L of air at 22.0 °C. It is warmed to 95.0 °C at constant pressure. What is its new
volume?

Convert to kelvins first: $T_1 = 295.15$ K and $T_2 = 368.15$ K.

$$
V_2 = V_1 \times \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 1.50\ \text{L} \times \frac{368.15\ \text{K}}{295.15\ \text{K}} = 1.87\ \text{L}
$$

Using Celsius would give $1.50 \times 95.0/22.0 = 6.48$ L, more than four times the volume, which can't be
right for a 73-degree change.

</Reveal>

## 4. Pressure and temperature (10.3)

At constant volume and amount, **pressure is proportional to absolute temperature**. This one is sometimes
called Gay-Lussac's law.

$$
\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2} \qquad (n, V \text{ constant})
$$

**Particle view:** the walls can't move, so faster particles simply hit them more often and harder. This is
why a sealed can or a car tire's pressure rises when it gets hot.

<Reveal label="Worked example: heating a sealed container">

A sealed can holds gas at 2.00 atm and 20 °C. It is left in a car that reaches 120 °C. What is the
pressure inside?

$$
P_2 = P_1 \times \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 2.00\ \text{atm} \times \frac{393.15\ \text{K}}{293.15\ \text{K}} = 2.68\ \text{atm}
$$

The Celsius mistake, $2.00 \times 120/20$, gives 12.0 atm.

</Reveal>

## 5. Avogadro's law: volume and amount (10.3)

At constant temperature and pressure, **volume is proportional to the number of moles**:

$$
\frac{V_1}{n_1} = \frac{V_2}{n_2} \qquad (P, T \text{ constant})
$$

Put another way, **equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of
particles**, whatever the gas. A liter of $\ce{H2}$ and a liter of $\ce{CO2}$ at the same conditions have
the same number of molecules, even though the $\ce{CO2}$ is 22 times heavier.

**Particle view:** more particles means more collisions, so at constant pressure the gas takes up more room.

<Reveal label="Worked example: Avogadro's law">

A cylinder with a movable piston holds 0.200 mol of gas in 4.00 L. Another 0.100 mol is added at the same
temperature and pressure. What is the new volume?

$$
V_2 = V_1 \times \frac{n_2}{n_1} = 4.00\ \text{L} \times \frac{0.300\ \text{mol}}{0.200\ \text{mol}} = 6.00\ \text{L}
$$

</Reveal>

## 6. The combined gas law

When $n$ stays the same but $P$, $V$, and $T$ all change, combine the laws:

$$
\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}
$$

This is on the equation sheet. Any one of the laws above is this equation with one variable crossed out
on both sides.

<Reveal label="Worked example: correcting a volume to STP">

A gas sample has a volume of 500. mL at 25 °C and 745 torr. What volume would it have at STP (273.15 K and
1 atm, or 760 torr)?

Solve for $V_2$ and check each ratio's direction:

$$
V_2 = V_1 \times \frac{P_1}{P_2} \times \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 500.\ \text{mL} \times \frac{745\ \text{torr}}{760\ \text{torr}} \times \frac{273.15\ \text{K}}{298.15\ \text{K}} = 449\ \text{mL}
$$

The pressure goes up (745 → 760 torr), which shrinks the gas, so the pressure ratio is less than 1. The
temperature goes down, which also shrinks it, so the temperature ratio is less than 1 too. Pressure units
don't need converting here, as long as both are the same unit.

</Reveal>

## 7. Reading gas-law graphs (AP topic 3.4)

The AP course description expects you to read and sketch graphs of these relationships.

![Six small graphs. Boyle's law: P against V is a curve that falls steeply and then levels off; P against 1/V is a straight line through the origin. Avogadro's law: V against n is a straight line through the origin. Charles's law: V against temperature in °C gives straight lines for different amounts of gas that all extend back to zero volume at −273 °C; V against temperature in kelvins is a straight line through the origin. At constant volume, P against temperature in kelvins is a straight line through the origin.](./gas-law-graphs.svg)

| Held constant | Relationship | Graph |
|---|---|---|
| $n$, $T$ | $P$ and $V$ inversely proportional | $P$ vs $V$ curves down; $P$ vs $1/V$ is a line through 0 |
| $n$, $P$ | $V \propto T$ (K) | Line through 0 in kelvins; reaches 0 at −273 °C in Celsius |
| $n$, $V$ | $P \propto T$ (K) | Line through 0 in kelvins |
| $P$, $T$ | $V \propto n$ | Line through 0 |

A straight line through the origin means the two quantities are **directly proportional**: doubling one
doubles the other. A straight line that doesn't pass through the origin, like $V$ against °C, means they
are related linearly but not proportional.

<Callout type="warning">
The most common gas-law mistake is leaving temperature in °C. The second is getting a ratio upside down.
After you set up a calculation, ask whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than the starting value,
and check that your ratio is greater or less than 1 to match.
</Callout>
