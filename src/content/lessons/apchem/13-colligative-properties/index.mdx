A **colligative property** depends on the *number* of dissolved solute particles, not on what those
particles are. A tablespoon of sugar and a tablespoon of salt affect water differently mainly because
they put different numbers of particles into solution.

## Counting particles: the van 't Hoff factor

The van 't Hoff factor $i$ is the number of particles each formula unit produces in solution.

| Solute | Dissolves as | Ideal $i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$ | molecules | 1 |
| $\ce{NaCl}$ | $\ce{Na+ + Cl-}$ | 2 |
| $\ce{CaCl2}$ | $\ce{Ca^2+ + 2Cl-}$ | 3 |

<Callout type="warning">
Measured values of $i$ for ionic compounds are a bit smaller than the ideal values, because some
oppositely charged ions stay paired in solution. Use the ideal value unless you are given a measured one.
</Callout>

## Molality

Colligative formulas use **molality**, moles of solute per kilogram of *solvent*:

$$
m = \frac{\text{mol solute}}{\text{kg solvent}}
$$

Molality does not change with temperature, which is why it is used here instead of molarity.

## The four properties

**Vapor-pressure lowering** (Raoult's law): a nonvolatile solute lowers the mole fraction of the
solvent, and the solvent's vapor pressure drops in proportion. $P_\text{solution} = X_\text{solvent}\,P^\circ_\text{solvent}$.

**Boiling-point elevation:** $\Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m$. For water, $K_b = 0.51\ ^\circ\text{C}/m$.

**Freezing-point depression:** $\Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m$. For water, $K_f = 1.86\ ^\circ\text{C}/m$.

**Osmotic pressure:** $\Pi = iMRT$, where $M$ is molarity, $R = 0.08206\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$, and $T$ is in kelvins.

<Reveal label="Worked example: freezing point of a glucose solution">

18.0 g of glucose ($\ce{C6H12O6}$, 180.16 g/mol) is dissolved in 250. g of water. Find the freezing point.

1. Moles of glucose: $18.0 / 180.16 = 0.0999\ \text{mol}$
2. Molality: $0.0999\ \text{mol} / 0.250\ \text{kg} = 0.400\ m$
3. $\Delta T_f = (1)(1.86)(0.400) = 0.744\ ^\circ\text{C}$
4. Freezing point: $0.00 - 0.744 = -0.744\ ^\circ\text{C}$

At the same molality, $\ce{CaCl2}$ (ideal $i = 3$) would lower the freezing point about three times as much, by roughly $2.23\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

</Reveal>
